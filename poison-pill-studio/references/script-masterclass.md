# Script masterclass: writing "wow", tight, funny rise-and-fall scripts (v5)

**How this file plugs into the pipeline:**
- **Director package §0** holds the one-page template (§9 below): THESIS ≤ 25 words, master loop, best fact (held for the
  midpoint), second-best fact (the hook), loop log and callback plant.
- **§3 SCRIPT is an A/V script** (§6 below), with these marks:
  - **hit word** in `**bold**`, one per sentence;
  - `[BEAT]` for a held pause, which the voice actually pauses on;
  - `(J)` for an intended laugh;
  - `[SFX: …]` and `[TEXT: …]`.
- **`pp_scriptlint --long`** checks:
  - hook and promise in 30 s;
  - something new every 40 s;
  - one laugh every 45–90 s;
  - two or more "in the room" scenes;
  - re-hooks;
  - but/therefore density;
  - specifics per minute;
  - no filler or hype words;
  - lines ≤ 20 words, final line ≤ 15 words, thesis ≤ 25 words.

  Score ≥ 8.5 before anything is drawn.
- **The critic** (gauntlet.md) uses the line-by-line codes and the 40-point scorecard in §7. Ship at ≥ 32/40.

All example lines are original, written in the style of these channels. They are not quotes. Treat any facts in them as placeholders and verify them before use.

## 0. The core model: a video is a chain of questions

Curiosity is the felt gap between what the viewer knows and what they want to know (Loewenstein, 1994). The gap must be **specific** (they can name the missing piece) and **not too big** (they believe it can be answered). A great script never lets that gap close without opening a new, bigger one first.

> **Retention = (open question) × (belief it will be answered soon) × (pleasure of the line right now)**

Every section of this guide pulls one of those three levers.

## 1. Hooks (0–30 s) and the Promise

### What the data-driven people say
- **MrBeast handbook:** the first minute is where most viewers are lost (one video lost about 21M viewers in the first minute out of about 60M clicks, and that counted as *good*). The first minute must prove the thumbnail and title promise **immediately**. The "wow" moment should come early; their example is a house craned in at about 30 s.
- **Paddy Galloway:** the outsized returns are in intros. "Aim to hold an extra 10% at the 30 second mark." Compare retention against similar videos, not in isolation.
- **Kallaway (secondary summaries):** a hook needs **topic clarity** plus **on-target curiosity**. Formula: **Context lean-in, then scroll-stop interjection ("but…"), then contrarian snapback.** Four hook killers: **delay, confusion, irrelevance, disinterest.** Visual, spoken and on-screen-text hooks should all say the same thing.

### The 30-second anatomy (business rise and fall)
| Time | Beat | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 s | **Cold open image + jolt line** | Confirm the title. Start mid-action. No "Hey guys", no logo. |
| 5–15 s | **Context lean-in** | Who, what, how big. One number that makes the scale physical. |
| 15–22 s | **"But" turn** | The contradiction that makes the story a story. |
| 22–30 s | **Promise + stakes** | What the viewer will understand by the end, and why it matters to *them*. |

### 7 hook formulas, each with an original example

1. **Peak-then-cliff (in medias res).** Open on the highest point, then the fall.
   > "In 2000, Blockbuster had 9,000 stores, 60,000 employees, and a chance to buy Netflix for $50 million. They laughed. Literally laughed. Ten years later, they were bankrupt."
2. **The impossible number.** A figure that should not be true.
   > "This company lost $1.6 million a day. Every day. For six years. And investors kept giving them more."
3. **The single object / room.** A physical detail that holds the whole story.
   > "There's a warehouse in New Jersey with 40,000 unsold juicers in it. Each one cost $400. Each one squeezes a bag you could squeeze with your hands."
4. **The contrarian snapback.** State the common belief, then break it.
   > "Everyone says Kodak died because it ignored digital. But Kodak *invented* the digital camera. In 1975. That's the problem."
5. **The countdown / clock.** Stakes with a timer.
   > "On Friday, the company had $4 billion. By Monday morning, it had a website that said 'temporarily unavailable.'"
6. **The character with a flaw.** A person, not a company.
   > "He wore the same black turtleneck, spoke in a borrowed baritone, and had never actually finished building the product. He was 31, and worth nine billion dollars."
7. **The question the viewer didn't know they had.**
   > "Why does every airport sell the same three brands of neck pillow? It turns out one man decided that. And then he lost everything."

### The Promise (the contract)
Say explicitly, around 20–30 s in, what the viewer gets. It is the "therefore you should stay" line.
- **Formula:** "This is the story of how [X] went from [peak] to [ruin], and the one decision in [year] that made it inevitable."
- **Stakes formula:** "…and the same mistake is being made right now by [company the viewer uses]."

**Do:** match the thumbnail's image in the first 3 s; use one big number; use a name; put a verb in the first sentence.
**Don't:** "In today's video…", "Before we start, subscribe…", channel intros, explaining background before the conflict, or more than one idea in the hook.

## 2. Structure and pacing

### Macro-structure for an 8–12 minute rise and fall
A five-act shape that fits most of the business channels (observed in MagnatesMedia, Moon, Business Casual, Bloomberg Originals):

| % of runtime | 10-min timestamp | Act | Must contain |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5% | 0:00–0:30 | **Cold open** | Peak or collapse image, contradiction, promise |
| 5–25% | 0:30–2:30 | **Origin / the bet** | Founder, the insight, the first unfair advantage. End on a cliffhanger. |
| 25–50% | 2:30–5:00 | **The rise** | Escalating wins, each bigger. Plant the flaw (Chekhov's gun). **Re-hook at about 3:00.** |
| ~50% | 5:00 | **Midpoint twist** | Revelation that reframes the rise ("the growth was fake", "the rival saw it coming"). |
| 50–85% | 5:00–8:30 | **The fall** | Cascading consequences, "but/therefore" chain, a desperate pivot. **Re-hook at about 6:00.** |
| 85–100% | 8:30–10:00 | **Aftermath + thesis** | Where they are now, callback to the opening image, the lesson in one sentence. |

The MrBeast handbook explicitly calls for **re-engagement beats at about 3 min and about 6 min**: highly interesting, story-relevant moments placed where viewers tend to get bored. Minutes 1–3 should move fast from hype into "the thing is happening".

### Reveal cadence (how often something new must happen)
- **Every 20–40 s:** a micro-turn. A new fact, number, joke or visual that changes the picture.
- **Every 60–90 s:** a mini-reveal that answers one question and opens another. This is a loop handoff.
- **Every 2.5–3 min:** a structural escalation such as an act break, a re-hook or a new character.
- No section should run more than about 3 min without a format change: a map, a clip, a chart, a cold quote, or a music drop.

### Open-loop rules
1. **Open before you close.** End each section by opening the next question *before* resolving the current one. ("They'd solved distribution. What they hadn't solved was the guy in Room 4.")
2. **Nest loops.** Keep one big loop for the whole video (from the hook), 2–3 medium loops (one per act) and many micro loops.
3. **Pay every loop.** An unpaid tease feels like clickbait and trains viewers to leave. Log each one: *opened at 0:45, paid at 6:10.*
4. **Name the delay honestly.** "We'll get to the $40 million yacht. First, you need to understand the spreadsheet."

### The reveal ladder
Order your facts from least to most shocking, so each rung tops the previous one. Hold the single best fact for the midpoint or the 6-minute re-hook, not the intro. The intro gets the *second* best (it needs to be good enough) plus a hint of the best one.

### Cliffhanger lines before act breaks (original examples)
> "It was the best quarter in the company's history. It would also be the last one they'd report honestly."

> "And for about eleven months, that worked."

> "There was just one problem. His name was Gary, and he had read the footnotes."

> "Everything was going perfectly. Which, in a video titled 'The Collapse of…', is never a good sign."

### "But / Therefore", not "And then" (Parker and Stone, NYU)
If you can put "and then" between two beats, the story is a list. Every beat should be caused by the previous one (*therefore*) or should complicate it (*but*).
- ❌ "They opened in Texas. And then they opened in Florida. And then they IPO'd."
- ✅ "They opened in Texas, *but* margins were razor-thin, *therefore* they needed scale fast, *therefore* they IPO'd early, *but* now Wall Street wanted 40% growth a year, *forever*."

**Test:** read your outline aloud with only the connecting words. You should hear something like "but… therefore… but… but… therefore…"

## 3. Line craft

### Specificity beats adjectives
Abstract statements slide past the viewer. Concrete ones stick. Replace every adjective with a fact.
| Abstract ❌ | Specific ✅ |
|---|---|
| "The company grew really fast." | "They went from one store to 1,100 in 36 months, about one new store a day." |
| "He was very wealthy." | "He owned four jets, and named all of them after himself." |
| "The product had quality issues." | "One in five units caught fire. The company called this 'thermal events'." |
| "Investors were excited." | "SoftBank wired $4.4 billion after a 28-minute tour." |

**Specificity checklist for each fact:** exact number, date, place (city, building, room), named person, physical object, sensory detail.

**Make numbers physical.** Translate scale into something visible: "$1.6M a day, which is a new Ferrari every 4 hours." Use one comparison per big number, not three.

### Write for the picture (and let the picture talk)
- Every line should imply a shot. If the editor can't picture it, rewrite it.
- **Don't narrate what's on screen; add to it.** If the chart shows revenue falling 80%, the voiceover says "and then everyone checked their emails," not "revenue fell 80%."
- Show documents, headlines, emails and tweets **on screen** and read only the damning 5 words aloud. (Coffeezilla's and Vox's style is heavy on documents, maps and diagrams. Wikipedia notes Coffeezilla's animated money-flow graphics.)
- Use visual metaphor for abstract mechanisms (Kurzgesagt plans visual metaphors right after the script is locked).

### Rhythm
- **Mix sentence lengths.** Long setup, then a short punch. "Long, long, short."
- **Punch line goes last.** Put the surprising word at the very end of the sentence. ("He'd built a $3 billion company out of a single, unpatented… sandwich.")
- **One idea per sentence.** Narration is heard, not read. If you need a comma to breathe, split it.
- **The fragment.** "Eleven billion dollars. Gone. In a weekend."
- **Rule of three, with the third broken.** "He had the vision, the funding, and absolutely no idea what a balance sheet was."
- **Contrast pairs.** "On paper: a unicorn. In the warehouse: a donkey."
- **Read it aloud at speed** (about 150–160 wpm, so 8 min is about 1,250 words and 12 min about 1,850). Any line you stumble on gets cut.

### Humor (dry, documentary-safe)
Observed across MagnatesMedia, Business Casual, Wendover, PolyMatter and Barely Sociable: the narrator stays calm while the facts are absurd. The joke comes from **understatement against escalation**.
1. **Deadpan understatement:** "This was, by most accounts, not ideal."
2. **The literal reading:** "The company described its strategy as 'aggressive'. That was accurate. It was mostly aggressive toward its own money."
3. **The aside / parenthetical:** "The board, which included three former senators and zero people who'd ever sold a mattress, approved it unanimously."
4. **The callback:** set up a detail early (the CEO's gold-plated stapler) and pay it off at the collapse ("The stapler sold at auction for $310.").
5. **Ironic visual cut (write it in the A/V column):** VO: "He assured investors everything was under control." VISUAL: stock footage of a dumpster fire.
6. **Expectation flip:** "They had a plan to win back customers. The plan was a mascot."
7. **Meta-narrator wink, used sparingly:** "If you're thinking 'surely someone stopped this' — no. No one did."

**Humor rules:** about one laugh beat every 45–90 s; never joke about victims or the people who lost jobs and savings; punch up at decisions and egos; the joke must also carry information (a joke that doesn't advance the story is a pause); cut any joke that needs explaining.

## 4. Scene writing: put the viewer in the room

Johnny Harris, Bloomberg Originals and LEMMiNO (observed) turn key moments into **scenes**: a specific time, place and person, a sensory detail, and a decision under pressure. Summary covers the boring years. Scenes cover the turning points.

**Scene formula:** **[Timestamp] + [Place, down to the room] + [Who's there] + [One sensory/physical detail] + [What's at stake in the next 60 seconds] + [The line or action that changes everything].**

Original examples:
> "9:14 a.m., March 3rd, 2008. A conference room on the 31st floor in Manhattan. The coffee is still warm. On the table: a single sheet of paper with one number circled in red."

> "It's 2 a.m. in a strip-mall office in Phoenix. Three engineers, one working prototype, and a demo for investors in seven hours. The prototype works about half the time."

> "The email arrives at 4:52 on a Friday, which, as anyone who has worked in an office knows, is when bad news is legally required to arrive."

**Scene rules:**
- Use present tense for scenes and past tense for summary. The tense switch itself signals that we're zooming in.
- Use one or two scenes per act, maximum. Scenes are expensive in screen time and visuals.
- Only invent nothing. If you lack details, use what is documented ("According to court filings, the meeting lasted 11 minutes"). Attribution is itself a texture.
- End the scene on the decision, then cut out.

## 5. Endings that land

The last 60–90 s decide comments, shares and whether viewers trust your next title.

**Ending structure:**
1. **Where are they now** (fast, factual, often wryly funny): "Today the brand is owned by a holding company in Delaware that also owns a regional mattress chain and a domain name."
2. **Callback to the cold-open image.** Close the biggest loop with the same visual. "That warehouse in New Jersey? It's a self-storage facility now. Unit 1140 still has a juicer in it."
3. **Thesis in one sentence**, the "so what" that applies beyond this company:
   > "Blockbuster didn't lose to better technology. It lost to its own late fees, the one part of the business customers hated and shareholders loved."
4. **Resonant final line**: short, concrete, a little ironic. Don't summarize the whole video.
   > "They were so busy being the biggest that they forgot to be the best. And it turns out you can only be one of those for free."

**The thesis test:** you should be able to write the thesis *before* drafting, in 25 words or fewer, as "X failed/rose because Y, which means Z." Every act should be evidence for it.

**Don't:** end on "So what do you think? Let me know in the comments" as the last line; introduce new facts in the final minute; moralize for more than two sentences; let music fade over a long sign-off (viewers leave the moment they sense the end, so put the end-screen CTA *inside* the final story beat or after the thesis, in under 10 s).

## 6. Scripting for visuals and sound

### The A/V (two-column) script
The industry-standard documentary and explainer format: **VIDEO** on the left (b-roll, archival, graphics, maps, on-screen text) and **AUDIO** on the right (VO, sync clips, SFX, music cues). An optional third column holds timing. Kurzgesagt's process locks the script and then designs about 200 visual panels from it. Write the visual intent *while* you write, not after.

Recommended column layout for this format:

| # | TIME | VISUAL | VO (hit word in **bold**) | SFX / MUSIC | ON-SCREEN TEXT |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 | 3:02 | Slow push on 2004 annual report cover | "By 2004 they were making **$6 billion** a year." | Music: confident pulse | $6,000,000,000 |
| 13 | 3:07 | Cut to: tiny news clipping | "But one line on page 47…" | **Music drops out** | — |
| 14 | 3:10 | Zoom on highlighted footnote | "…said **something else**." | Single low hit | "Page 47" |
| 15 | 3:13 | Black | (2 s silence) | Room tone | — |

### Marking conventions (adopt these in every script)
- **Bold** = the hit word, where the cut, graphic pop or SFX lands. One per sentence, maximum.
- `[BEAT]` = a 0.5–1 s pause for a joke or reveal to land.
- `[DROP]` = music cuts out; the next line is delivered over silence or room tone. Use it before the biggest reveals (about 3–5 per video).
- `[SWELL]` = music returns or builds, typically on a "therefore" that escalates.
- `[SFX: cash register]` = written in the script, not left to the editor.
- `[TEXT]` = verbatim on-screen text (numbers, quotes, document lines).
- `[ARCHIVAL]` / `[MAP]` / `[CHART]` / `[DOC]` = asset type, so research knows what to source.

### Sound-design-aware writing
- **Silence is the loudest reveal.** Script the drop *before* the line, not on it.
- **Music changes at act breaks**, the musical equivalent of a chapter title. Rise = driving and optimistic; midpoint = a sudden key change or stop; fall = sparse; ending = a reprise of the opening motif (an audio callback).
- **Write lines to be cut on.** Short sentences give the editor cut points. A 40-word sentence over one shot kills pacing.
- **Comedic timing lives in the edit:** script the `[BEAT]` and the ironic visual explicitly, or the editor will smooth it away.

## 7. Critic's checklist / scoring rubric

### A. Line-by-line pass (mark each line)
| Code | Question | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **S** | Is it specific (number, name, date, place, object)? | Replace the adjective with a fact |
| **P** | Can I picture the shot? | Add a visual or rewrite as an image |
| **R** | Does it repeat what the visual already shows? | Cut it, or add a new layer |
| **B/T** | Does it connect by "but" or "therefore"? | Re-order or add causality |
| **E** | Does the important word come at the end? | Restructure the sentence |
| **L** | Under about 20 words? Readable in one breath? | Split it |
| **J** | Is it filler/jargon ("in today's world", "it's important to note")? | Delete it |
| **Q** | Does it open, advance, or pay off a question? | If none, cut it |

**Target:** at least 90% of lines pass S or P; zero "and then" transitions between beats; zero J lines.

### B. Structural scorecard (score 0–5 each, 40 max; ship at 32 or above)
1. **Hook (0–30 s):** matches title/thumbnail in 5 s, one big number, a contradiction, promise stated by 30 s.
2. **Stakes:** do I know why it matters to *me*, the viewer?
3. **Loop economy:** one master loop, 2–3 act loops, every loop paid off (with a loop log).
4. **Escalation cadence:** a new fact/turn every 20–40 s; re-hooks at about 3:00 and 6:00; the midpoint twist reframes the story.
5. **Causality:** the outline reads as a but/therefore chain.
6. **Scenes:** at least 2 fully realized "in the room" scenes at the turning points.
7. **Humor:** a laugh beat every 45–90 s, deadpan, informative, punching up, with at least 1 callback.
8. **Ending:** callback to the opening image, a thesis of 25 words or fewer, a final line under 15 words, nothing new in the last minute.

### C. Quick red-flag list
- More than 15 s before the first conflict
- Two consecutive paragraphs of background with no question open
- Any number without a comparison that makes it physical
- The same sentence length three times in a row
- Superlatives ("incredible", "insane", "shocking") doing the work facts should do
- An act break that ends on a resolution instead of a question
- A joke at a victim's expense
- A final minute that summarizes instead of landing

### D. Retention-graph diagnosis (after publishing)
- **Steep drop in 0–30 s:** hook or promise mismatch. Rewrite the first 3 lines and check them against the thumbnail.
- **Cliff at a specific timestamp:** find the line, visual or transition there (usually a background dump, a sponsor read or a resolved loop).
- **Slow slide through the middle:** cadence problem. Add reveals, cut the "and then" beats, move the best fact later.
- **Spike:** that's a rewatch or a skip-to. Learn what the viewer wanted, and give it earlier next time.

## 8. Bonus: 12 more original style-example lines

1. "On paper, it was the fastest-growing restaurant chain in America. In practice, it was a very expensive way to sell lukewarm soup."
2. "The plan had three steps. Step one: raise $200 million. Step two was unclear. Step three: profit."
3. "Inside the company, this was known as 'Project Phoenix'. Which, in hindsight, assumes there's going to be a fire."
4. "He didn't just ignore the warning. He promoted the guy who sent it — to an office in Anchorage."
5. "For a brief moment in 2015, this was worth more than Ford. It had sold 900 cars."
6. "The auditors asked to see the inventory. The inventory, it turned out, was a PowerPoint."
7. "Revenue was up 300%. Costs were up 900%. You don't need a finance degree for this part."
8. "And that's when the phone rang. Not a metaphor. An actual phone, in an actual kitchen, at 6:40 in the morning."
9. "Every expert said it couldn't scale. They were wrong. It scaled beautifully — straight into a wall."
10. "This is the moment the story stops being about a product. It becomes about a lie, and how long you can keep one alive."
11. "They had one job: don't run out of cash. They ran out of cash on a Tuesday."
12. "Remember the gold stapler? It's about to become important."

## 9. One-page template (paste into each new script)

```
TITLE / THUMBNAIL PROMISE:
THESIS (≤25 words): X [rose/fell] because Y, which means Z.
MASTER LOOP (the question the whole video answers):
BEST FACT (held for midpoint / 6:00):     SECOND-BEST FACT (hook):
LOOP LOG: [opened @ — paid @ —] x N
CALLBACK PLANT (object/joke from act 1 paid at ending):

0:00 COLD OPEN – image / jolt line / "but" / promise
0:30 ORIGIN – founder, bet, unfair advantage → cliffhanger
2:30 RISE – escalating wins, plant flaw → RE-HOOK @3:00
5:00 MIDPOINT TWIST – reframe
5:00 FALL – but/therefore cascade, desperate pivot → RE-HOOK @6:00
8:30 AFTERMATH – where now / callback / thesis / final line (<15 words)
WORD BUDGET: ~150 wpm × minutes
```
