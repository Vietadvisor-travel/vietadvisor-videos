# Script Ultimate — the Vietadvisor retention playbook

For the writer model. Read once, then use sections 3, 4, 6 and 7 as you draft every episode.
Researched Oct 2026. Numbers come in three grades. **[M]** = measured (a study or the creator's own data). **[R]** = an
analyst's or creator's rule of thumb. **[V]** = a vendor claim with no stated method. Treat [V] as a direction, not a target.
Every Vietnam fact in the example lines below is illustrative. **Verify each one before it goes into a script.**

---

## 0. The diagnosis in one paragraph

Visuals at 7/10 plus a bland script means the script is a list of true statements joined by "and then". It holds no
open question, no stakes and no turns. Viewers never stop to think "wait, what?", and nothing in the next second costs
them anything to miss. The fix is structural before it is stylistic. Every beat must (a) answer one question while opening
a new one, (b) join to the next beat with **but** or **therefore** (Trey Parker), and (c) raise the cost of leaving. The
ep4 "five rules" vertical shows the failure: Rule 1, Rule 2… Rule 5 is a textbook *and-then* chain. Its rules are good,
but they need to become a **chain of consequences**: each rule is born from the last one failing.

## 1. The retention model, window by window

The MrBeast production memo opens the topic this way: "The first minute of each video is the most important minute." In
one of his videos, ~21 M of ~60 M viewers left in the first minute **[M]**. "If we can get them to watch the first half of
the video there's a very high chance they'll watch to the end." **[M]**

| Window | What the viewer is deciding | What kills it | Numbers | Script fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0–1 s (vertical)** | "Is this for me / is this weird enough to stop?" | logo, greeting, a black or slow first frame, music before words, a static zoom | VVSA (viewed vs swiped) under 60% = "probably in trouble". Top Shorts land at 70–90% **[M, Galloway, 5,400 Shorts / 3.3 B views]**. "Treat your intro like a thumbnail." | First spoken word ≤ 0.5 s. The most striking frame of the film comes first. On-screen text by 0.5 s. |
| **1–3 s** | "Is there a question I now need answered?" | no tension, a topic stated without a stake, more than one promise | 63% of TikTok's highest-CTR ads put the key message in the first 3 s **[M, TikTok]**. 50–60% of all drop-off happens in the first 3 s **[V, Opus]**. | Context lean → interjection → snapback (Kallaway, §2). One promise only. |
| **3–15 s** ("investment window") | "Is the promise real?" | restating the hook, slow establishing context, a "today we'll…" preview | Skipping the confirmation beat loses 30–50% of would-be stayers **[V, Shortzly]**. If the best point waits until 15 s, ~70% are already gone **[V]**. | A first payoff (a "hit of value") by 5–8 s, then open the bigger loop. |
| **15–30 s** | "Is this going anywhere?" | a static shot held for 12+ s, no new information, a flat tone | Static frames lose viewers after ~12–15 s **[V]**. A YouTube "Intro" retention (viewers still watching at 30 s) above 50% = outperforming **[R]**. | Something new every 5–8 s: new information, a new image, a new character, a turn. |
| **30–60 s dip** (vertical) / **25–35 s** (YouTube) | Drifters leave once the first loop feels "answered" | the opening loop paid off with nothing bigger behind it | YouTube: put a pattern interrupt at 25–35 s, "when new viewers tend to drift" **[R, air.io]**. The quarter-mark dip is the most common mid-video drop **[R, prepublish]**. | **Re-hook #1** must arrive *before* the first payoff resolves: "That's the easy part. The hard part is…" |
| **1–3 min (YouTube)** | "Am I invested in the story?" | telling instead of showing, a slow chronology | MrBeast: "Stop telling people what they will be watching and start showing them." Cover a lot of story fast ("multiple days in the first 3 minutes"). **[M]** | Compress the timeline. Get to the first big event early. Re-hook around 3:00. |
| **Mid-video (3–6 min)** | "Still worth it?" | lingering after a payoff, an essay passage with no question running | Lingering after a payoff is "the number one place apart from the hook where people will lose interest" **[R, G. Blackman]**. MrBeast puts a "highly interesting" re-engagement at ~6:00 **[M]**. | Setup → tension → payoff every 1–2 min. Escalate stakes every 60–90 s **[R]**. Midpoint twist at ~50%. |
| **Last 20%** | "It's ending; I can leave." | a summary, "so that's…", "thanks for watching", a slow fade, a long outro | A "cliff at the end" comes from an outro with nothing left to gain **[R]**. MrBeast: "Don't ever signal the end… endings must always be abrupt." **[M]** | Keep the strongest payoff here, never a recap. End on a button line plus a cut, or a loop back to frame 1 (vertical). |

### Benchmarks (use as orientation; always compare against our own past episodes)
- **Shorts average view-through**: median ~45%, top 10% ~91% **[V-plus: quso 508 K posts]**. "Strong" by length: under 15 s
  >75%, 15–30 s >65%, 30–60 s >55%, 60 s–3 min >45% (TikTok) **[R, Retensis]**.
- **YouTube average percentage viewed**: 5–10 min "solid" 45%+, "strong" 60%+ **[R]**. An extra 10 points of retention "can be
  the difference between 100k and 1 million views" (Galloway) **[R]**.
- **Loops** only work well under ~25–35 s **[R]**. Our vertical cut is 60–150 s, so use *narrative callbacks*, not
  seamless loops. Since 31 Mar 2025, a Shorts replay counts as a view **[M, YouTube]**.
- **Sharing** (Berger & Milkman, ~7,000 NYT articles) **[M]**: +1 SD in awe raises the chance of going "most-emailed" by
  ~30%, anger ~34%, practical utility ~30%, interest ~25%, surprise ~14%. Sadness *lowers* it ~16%. High-arousal feelings travel;
  low-arousal ones don't. For us: **awe + usefulness + surprise**, never wallowing sadness.

### The three laws that fall out of the data
1. **Every second must earn the next second.** The MrBeast team's version: "make the next second more interesting than the last."
2. **Never resolve a loop without another one already open** (overlap the loops like roof tiles).
3. **Never signal the end.** No recap, no outro language. The last line is a payoff, not a goodbye.

## 2. Hook engineering

### 2.1 Anatomy: the Kallaway 3-step (first 3–6 s)
1. **Context lean**: name the topic in plain words so the right viewer leans in. Add common ground, a benefit/pain, or a surprising number.
2. **Scroll-stop interjection**: one line, usually starting **but / yet / however / except**. It is the "stun gun".
3. **Contrarian snapback**: go the *opposite* way from the lean while staying on topic. "The bigger the shock, the stronger the snap."
   The payoff has to be real, or viewers churn.

Worked example (ep4): *"Hà Nội has seven million motorbikes." (lean) "And the trick to crossing isn't courage." (interjection) "It's being boring." (snapback)*

### 2.2 The triple: visual + text + voice, aligned
Kallaway: the **spoken hook is only the third most important part**. The visual hook and the written hook come first, and
all three must say the *same* thing in different ways (alignment beats cleverness).

| Channel | Rule | Example (Bạch Đằng) |
|---|---|---|
| **Visual (0.0 s)** | The single most striking frame of the episode, already in motion. No title card, no logo. | A Mongol warship impaled on a stake as the tide drops. |
| **Text (≤ 0.5 s)** | 3–5 big words. It should *complement* the voice (number, stake, contradiction), not echo every word. | "THE RIVER WAS A TRAP" |
| **Voice (≤ 0.5 s start)** | 6–12 words, present tense, one promise. | "The Mongols conquered China. Then they met this river." |

Test: mute it. Does the visual + text alone still create the question? Then close your eyes. Does the voice alone? Both must pass.

### 2.3 The promise line
By 5–8 s (vertical) or 15–30 s (YouTube), say in one sentence what the viewer gets by staying. Make it a **promise, not a
process**: no "first we'll look at…". Allow one credibility point at most (G. Blackman). Never put a metaphor in the hook.
"If you need a metaphor to explain your hook, you've already lost."
- Travel: *"By the end, you'll cross like a local, and know the one vehicle that never stops."*
- History: *"Here's how a tiny kingdom beat the biggest empire on Earth, three times."*

### 2.5 Hook don'ts (any one = rewrite)
- A greeting, logo, channel name, "In this video", "Today we're going to", or a question the viewer can answer "no" to ("Ever been to Hà Nội?").
- Two or more promises. A metaphor. A date as the opening word ("In 1288…"). A fact with no stake ("Hà Nội is the capital").
- Revealing the answer in the hook. Clickbait the film can't pay off. MrBeast: viewers "feel like you were lied to and click off."
- A slow zoom on a still, or music before the first word. The text card should not repeat the voice word for word (complement it).

## 3. Structure templates

### 3.1 Narration budget
Kokoro `af_heart` reads at roughly 2.5 words/s. Budget **~2.2 words/s** once you count `…`/`—` beats, dialogue gaps and
silent moments. 60 s ≈ 130 words, 90 s ≈ 200, 150 s ≈ 330. YouTube 8 min ≈ 1,000–1,100 words. **Measure on our pipeline
and update this number.**

### 3.2 Vertical, 60–150 s ("90-second spine": scale the middle, never the hook or the ending)
| Time (90 s cut) | Beat | Job | Writing rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.0–0.5 | **Visual hook** | the strongest frame, already moving | Sound hits on frame 1 |
| 0.3–3 | **Verbal hook** (lean → interjection → snapback) | open Loop A (the big question) | ≤ 12 words; text card ≤ 5 words |
| 3–8 | **Promise + first hit of value** | prove the video is real | one concrete number or a tip *now* |
| 8–20 | **Setup with a problem** | protagonist + want + obstacle | introduce Dave/Kenji (the viewer's stand-in) with a stake |
| 20–28 | **Turn 1 (BUT)** | first attempt fails / the obvious answer is wrong | fail gag, consequence shown |
| 28–35 | **Re-hook #1** (before the 30–60 s dip) | open Loop B *before* closing anything | "That's the easy part." / "Except there's a catch." |
| 35–55 | **Escalation (THEREFORE ×2)** | each beat a bigger version of the problem | stakes ladder (§5.2); new image every 5–8 s |
| 55–65 | **Midpoint twist** | the reveal that reframes Loop A | "The trick was never X. It was Y." |
| 65–78 | **Payoff of Loop B, then Loop A** (inner first, outer last) | the satisfying "oh!" | the saveable fact lives here |
| 78–86 | **Button / callback** | opening image or line returns, transformed | Dave crosses calmly; the stake from 0 s reappears |
| 86–90 | **End frame** | brand, 1.6 s | no "follow", no "thanks"; the CTA goes in the caption |

- **60 s**: cut to hook → promise → one turn → twist → payoff → button. Keep the twist.
- **150 s**: add a second escalation block (35–55 → 35–95) with **Re-hook #2 at ~60 s and #3 at ~100 s**, plus a dialogue
  scene. Turn something every 5–8 s **[R]**. A cut every 2–4 s **[V]** is for footage; our moments can hold 4–6 s if the
  *voice* turns inside them.
- **Loop-back**: at 60–150 s, don't use a seamless sentence loop. Do end on a frame that *rhymes* with frame 1 (same
  camera, new state) and a line that answers the first line. That rewards a rewatch without feeling cut off.

### 3.3 YouTube, 6–10 min (shown at 8:00; scale chapters, not the cold open)
| Time | Beat | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:05 | **Cold open, mid-action** | the most dramatic moment of the story (flash-forward) |
| 0:05–0:20 | **The question + stakes** | "How did X…?" Name what was at risk. |
| 0:20–0:40 | **Promise + 3 loop teasers** | "two invasions, a tattoo, a trap that needed the moon…" (promises, not process) |
| 0:40–0:50 | **Title sting** | ≤ 5 s; never a long intro |
| 0:50–2:00 | **Ch.1, the ordinary world + the threat** | protagonist, want, the giant. First payoff by ~1:30 (a small loop closes). |
| 2:00–2:15 | **Re-hook** | "And that was the *first* time." |
| 2:15–3:45 | **Ch.2, first clash (BUT)** | the obvious plan fails; a cost is paid (Story Circle "Search") |
| 3:45–4:00 | **Re-hook / stakes up** | new character or reversal, "Then Kublai sent his son." |
| 4:00–5:00 | **Midpoint (~50%)** | false victory or reframing reveal (Save the Cat midpoint). Plant the device for the finale here. |
| 5:00–6:15 | **Ch.3, bad guys close in** | stakes peak; "all is lost" around 75% (6:00) |
| 6:15–7:20 | **Finale** | the planted device pays off; the biggest image of the film |
| 7:20–7:45 | **Meaning + travel payoff** | what it means now: the place, the distance, what to see, the price |
| 7:45–8:00 | **Button + tease** | callback to the cold open, then one line teasing the next episode; cut hard to the end screen |

- **Setup → tension → payoff every 1–2 min** **[R, Blackman]**. Re-hooks every 60–90 s **[R]**. Stakes escalate every 60–90 s **[R]**.
  A visual change every 15–25 s for a young audience **[R]**. Story movements of ~2 min — never labelled as chapters on screen (house rule).
- **Value placement**: give a real hit of value in the first 60 s, but keep the *best* payoff in the last third.
  Never put "the not-as-good content" where it ends a chapter (MrBeast puts it in the back half, carried by loops).
- **End-screen tease**: the last spoken line opens a new loop for the *next* video ("But the river had one more secret.").
  Never a summary.

### 3.4 Open loops: how to open, hold and pay off
- **Open** a loop with a specific, unanswered question the viewer *now* cares about: "Why would a general wait for the tide?"
  Add a hint of the answer (Loewenstein: curiosity needs "intriguing but incomplete information").
- **Hold**: vertical 10–40 s, YouTube 1–4 min (Loop A can run the whole film). **Feed it once mid-hold** with a partial
  answer or a sharper question ("The answer was under the water."), or it goes cold.
- **Nest** at most 3 deep (A ⊃ B ⊃ C). **Close in reverse order**: C, then B, then A. The outer loop carries the emotion, the inner ones the facts.
- **Pay off** fully, specifically, with an image. Never pay off at the *start* of a segment. After a payoff, ≤ 1 beat
  of proof, then the next setup **immediately**. A loop left unpaid = a broken promise (it reads as clickbait).
- **Overlap**: open Loop B 3–10 s *before* Loop A's first partial payoff.

### 3.5 Re-hook phrasing bank (rotate; never the same one twice in an episode)
"That's the easy part." · "Except there's a catch." · "Which was a problem." · "And that's when it got worse." · "But that wasn't
the weird part." · "Here's what nobody tells you." · "Remember the stakes? This is why." · "Hold that thought." · "Then the tide
turned. Literally." · "Wrong." · "Almost." · "And then the king died." · "That should have been the end. It wasn't." · "Now
watch the bus." · a direct question: "Guess what he did next?" (followed by a 0.6 s beat, then an answer nobody guessed).

### 3.6 Turning a list into a chain (fix for every "N rules / N tips" script)
A list = *and then*. A chain = each item exists **because the last one wasn't enough**.
- And-then: "Rule 1: never run. Rule 2: no sudden stops. Rule 3: walk steadily."
- Chain: "Dave ran. **But** riders read your speed, and running breaks the math. **So** he froze. **But** freezing is worse:
  the bike behind you has nowhere to go. **Therefore** the only move is the boring one: slow, steady, every step the same."
Keep the numbered text cards (progress markers help retention), but the *voice* must link them with but/therefore.

### 3.7 Story engines (pick one per episode, stated in the treatment)
- **Story Circle (Harmon), for 1 protagonist**: You → Need → Go → Search → Find → **Take (pay a price)** → Return → Change. Vertical: one beat per 10 s.
- **Pixar spine for history**: "Once upon a time… Every day… One day… Because of that… Because of that… Until finally…"
  The "because of that" links are your *therefores*.
- **Save the Cat compressed (YouTube)**: Opening Image 1% · Catalyst 10% · Break into Two 20–30% · Midpoint 50% (false victory /
  raised stakes) · All Is Lost 75% · Finale 80–99% · Final Image (mirrors the opening).
- Existing structures in `references/script-craft.md` (Wrong way → right way, Mystery, Countdown with a turn…) still apply.
  Layer this playbook's loops and but/therefore on top.

## 4. Line-level writing rules

### 4.1 Rhythm and length
1. **Average sentence 8–12 words** (broadcast standard). Hook sentences ≤ 8. No sentence over 20 words, ever.
2. **Short–short–long.** Two punches, then a line that carries the image: "Bikes left. Bikes right. And somewhere in the middle, Dave,
   holding a bánh mì like a shield." Then reset with a 2–4-word line.
3. **Staccato early, longer later** (Kallaway). The hook is the most compressed part of the script.
4. **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has "and" joining two facts, split it.
5. **Vary the sentence openings.** No three sentences in a row may start with the same word, or with "The", "It", "This".
6. **Punchlines are ≤ 4 words** and come *last* in the sentence (and last in the beat). Put a `…` or `—` before them.

### 4.2 Concreteness and specificity
7. **Concrete over abstract**: "a 40-seat bus that won't brake" beats "large vehicles". Johnny Harris's pillars are
   "visual, plain, clear, concrete".
8. **Every abstract claim gets a picture within one line**: "Traffic is chaotic" → "Four bikes, one family, a fridge."
9. **Numbers: one per sentence, rounded, in human units.** Say "about two dollars", not "50,000 dong, approximately 1.97 USD".
   Then translate the scale: "seven million bikes, one for every person in the city".
10. **Names beat categories**: "Trần Hưng Đạo", not "a Vietnamese general"; "Cô Phở", not "a local".
11. **Sensory verbs**: hiss, slam, drift, drip, crack. Not "is", "has", "there are". Cut "there is/are" on sight.
12. **"If I see it, I should hear it."** The narration names what is on screen *now*: "these stakes", "that bus".

### 4.3 Cutting filler (delete on sight)
very, really, actually, basically, just, quite, incredibly, amazing, stunning, beautiful, breathtaking, hidden gem, must-see,
"it's worth noting", "interestingly", "in fact", "of course", "as you can see", "let's take a look", "so,", "now,", "well,".
Also any line that restates the previous line, any "in this video", and any sentence a viewer could predict word for word.

### 4.4 Logic and tension
13. **But/therefore test**: between every pair of beats you must be able to insert *but* or *therefore*. If only "and then"
    fits, cut the beat or reorder it.
14. **"And this, but that" (Blackman)**: state the common belief in ≤ 10 s, then contradict it at once.
15. **Stakes in every beat**: what is lost if this fails? Time, money, face, a life, a kingdom. Pixar #16: "Stack the odds against."
16. **Give the protagonist an opinion and a want** (Pixar #13: passive characters are "poison"). Dave *wants* phở before it sells out.
17. **Rule of three with a twist**: two normal items, the third absurd or reversing. "Bring cash, sunscreen… and an emotional
    support raincoat." A history version: "They had horses, siege engines, and the world's largest empire. Vietnam had mud."
18. **Questions you answer *later*, not now**: no question is answered in the same beat it is asked.

### 4.5 Making facts surprising
19. **Lead with the misconception** (Derek Muller's PhD: videos that state the misconception first produce more learning,
    while "clear" videos make viewers *feel* they learned when they didn't). "Most people think the Mongols lost to the
    weather. They lost to a calendar."
20. **Comparison to the familiar**: "Sơn Đoòng could fit a 40-storey building" (verify). "The bus is the only thing on the
    road that doesn't negotiate."
21. **Reveal the mechanism, not just the fact**: not "they used stakes" but "the stakes stood just below the high-tide line, so the
    fleet sailed over them at high water and landed on them at low".
22. **The "today years old" turn**: a small fact that recolours something the viewer already knows ("Phở is a breakfast").

### 4.6 Making the viewer feel smart
23. **Plant the clue before the reveal** so the viewer can almost solve it: show the tide chart at 0:40, pay it off at 1:10.
24. **Guess beats**: "Guess the price… lower… lower." The viewer commits, then is rewarded or surprised. One per vertical.
25. **Don't over-explain the joke or the reveal.** Cut the line after the punchline. Trust the image.
26. **The local knows; the tourist learns.** Dave/Kenji is the viewer's ignorance so the viewer never feels talked down to.

### 4.7 Plant and payoff
27. **Every planted object, line or character must return** (the tide, the horn, the bánh mì shield). If it doesn't return, cut it.
28. **Plant once lightly, once visibly, then pay off.** The payoff should *change the meaning* of the plant.
29. **Callback endings**: the final line answers or inverts the first line ("How do you cross…?" → "Like a buffalo.").
30. **Pixar #19**: coincidences can get characters *into* trouble, never out. The payoff must be earned by a plant.

### 4.8 Writing for the ear (Kokoro `af_heart`)
31. Kokoro keeps `; : , . ! ? — … " ( )`. It **drops** `~ @ # $ % & * / + = | < >` and symbols like `° × ™`. **Spell
    them out**: "fifty percent", "two to three", "dollars", "degrees".
32. **`…` = a held beat before a reveal; `—` = a turn mid-sentence.** Use ≤ 1 per sentence and ≤ 1 per 15 s, or the voice drags.
33. **Write numbers the way you want them read**: "seven million", "twelve eighty-eight" (not "1288" unless the pipeline
    reads years correctly; test it). Add a Vietnamese word only with its English gloss in the same sentence.
34. **Read every line aloud at speed.** Any stumble, tongue-twister ("steady stream of stopped scooters") or ambiguous stress gets
    rewritten. Question marks must sit on genuine questions (TTS raises the pitch).
35. **Front-load the subject; end on the stress word.** "The bus doesn't stop" → "Everything else stops. Not the bus."
36. **Present tense for story** ("The tide turns. The ships stop."). It is more urgent and easier on TTS than perfect tenses.

## 5. Emotional design

### 5.1 Where each emotion goes
| Emotion | Job | Placement (vertical / YouTube) | Device |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Curiosity** | the engine; never off | 0–3 s and every re-hook | the open question, a mystery object, a contradiction |
| **Tension** | makes leaving costly | 20–60 s / 2–6 min | a ticking clock, stakes ladder, protagonist in danger |
| **Humour** | releases tension so it can be rebuilt; buys patience | one gag per 15–25 s, always *right after* a dense or tense line | deadpan understatement, a fail gag, rule of three, a fourth-wall aside |
| **Surprise** | resets attention, drives shares (+14%) | the midpoint twist + each re-hook | reversal, misconception flip, scale flip |
| **Awe** | the peak; the most shareable (+30%) | ~65–75% of runtime, held 2–3 s with *less* narration | the big set piece, a slow camera move, music swell, one short line |
| **Delight / warmth** | rewards the viewer, earns the follow | payoff + button | a local's kindness, Dave succeeds, a callback |
| **Usefulness** | the saveable reason to share (+30%) | last 15% | the one-line rule, price, time, place |
Rule: **alternate high/low**: tension → laugh → tension → bigger tension → awe → warmth. Never two dense info beats back to back
without a gag, an image or a guess between them. Avoid low-arousal sadness. Tell tragedies plainly and briefly, then move.

### 5.2 Stakes ladders (each rung = one beat; climb ≥ 3 rungs)
**Travel: "Crossing the street" (90 s)**
1. *Inconvenience*: Dave can't reach the phở stall across the road (it sells out at 10).
2. *Embarrassment*: he waits five minutes; a grandmother with groceries crosses past him.
3. *Danger*: he runs, and a bike swerves. "Running breaks the math."
4. *Worse danger*: he freezes mid-road. The bike behind has nowhere to go.
5. *Irreversible*: a bus. "Everything negotiates. Except this."
6. *Twist*: the solution is the *least* brave move: be boring, slow, predictable.
7. *Payoff + button*: he crosses; the stall is closed; Cô Phở: "Tomorrow, cross earlier."

**History: "Bạch Đằng 1288" (YouTube 8 min; verify every fact)**
1. *Pressure*: the Yuan (Mongol) empire, the largest land empire in history, demands submission.
2. *Personal*: elders are asked "fight or surrender?" (Diên Hồng conference). Soldiers tattoo "Sát Thát" ("kill the Mongols") on their arms. There is no going back.
3. *Loss*: the capital falls and is abandoned. Retreat looks like defeat (BUT it is strategy).
4. *Clock*: the Yuan fleet must withdraw down the Bạch Đằng river. The tide is the clock.
5. *Plant*: a 350-year-old trick (Ngô Quyền, 938): stakes hidden under high water.
6. *Peak*: the bait retreat, the fleet follows, the tide falls, the ships are impaled. The awe set piece.
7. *Meaning*: the third invasion fails; the travel payoff is the stake field you can visit today (Quảng Ninh), with distance and time from Hạ Long.

### 5.3 The OverSimplified humour kit (our tone: deadpan narrator, petty motives, no punching down)
- **Modern understatement for big events**: "This was, diplomatically speaking, a no."
- **Anachronistic inner voice**: an emperor thinks in office-email language ("Per my last invasion…").
- **Petty, relatable motives** for empires and rulers. **Underreaction to disaster** ("Dude. …Not cool.").
- **Running gag with escalating callbacks** (the third envoy, "AGAIN?!").
- **Hard cut to a reaction** after a dense passage (Dave's face, no line, 1.5 s).
- Humour targets egos, plans and empires. **Never** ethnic groups, victims or locals' poverty.

## 6. Boring-script detector + rewrite procedure

### 6.1 The checklist (each item is yes/no; any "no" in the first 8 = rewrite before scoring)
1. Does the first spoken word land ≤ 0.5 s, and is frame 1 the strongest image of the film?
2. Does the hook contain a contradiction, stake, number or mystery, *and* avoid answering it?
3. Do voice, text card and visual say the same thing three different ways?
4. Is there exactly one promise, stated by 8 s (vertical) / 30 s (YouTube)?
5. Does the first hit of value arrive by 8 s / 60 s?
6. Can *but* or *therefore* sit between every pair of beats (zero "and then")?
7. Is Re-hook #1 placed before 30 s (vertical) / at 25–35 s and again at ~2:00 (YouTube)?
8. Does the ending avoid every recap, "so that's", "thanks", "hope you enjoyed", "follow for more"?
9. Does a named protagonist want something specific within 15 s / 60 s?
10. Do the stakes climb at least three rungs?
11. Is there a midpoint twist that reframes the opening question?
12. Are the loops nested ≤ 3 deep and closed in reverse order, all of them paid off?
13. Is every loop fed once while it is open?
14. Does something new (information, image, character, turn) arrive every 5–8 s (vertical) / 15–25 s (YouTube)?
15. Is there a gag at least every 25 s (vertical) / 60 s (YouTube), placed after dense lines?
16. Is there one awe moment with ≤ 1 short line over it?
17. Does each sentence average ≤ 12 words, with none over 20?
18. Is there at least one short–short–long run per 20 s?
19. Is every number rounded, single, and translated into a human comparison?
20. Is every abstract claim followed by a concrete picture?
21. Does the script contain zero banned filler words (§4.3) and zero travel clichés (hidden gem, must-see, stunning)?
22. Is at least one misconception stated and then flipped?
23. Is at least one clue planted before its reveal (a viewer could almost guess it)?
24. Does the viewer get one guess / participation beat?
25. Does every planted object or line return?
26. Does the last line call back to the first line or first image?
27. Is the saveable fact (rule / price / time / place) in the last 15%?
28. Does no line restate the previous line?
29. Is no question answered in the same beat it was asked?
30. Is no beat a fact without a stake ("X was built in Y")?
31. Does the read-aloud test pass with no stumbles, no unread symbols, and years and prices written as spoken?
32. Does the title/thumbnail promise match what the first 30 s delivers?
33. Would the script still make sense muted (captions + visuals) *and* with eyes closed (voice only)?

### 6.2 Rewrite procedure (apply to every flagged line)
1. **Name the line's job** (hook, setup, turn, payoff, button). If it has none, delete it.
2. **Find the hidden stake**: who loses what if this is ignored?
3. **Find the hidden contradiction**: what does the viewer currently believe that this line breaks?
4. **Make it concrete**: replace every abstract noun with an object, a person or a number.
5. **Write three versions**: (a) **contradiction** (lean + snapback), (b) **stakes** (loss/clock), (c) **image-first** (picture + 4-word punch).
6. **Cut 30%** of the words (Opus suggests cutting scripts ~30%). Move the stress word to the end.
7. **Read it aloud** with the line before and after. Keep the version that makes the *next* line necessary.

## 7. Scoring rubric (0–100). Score every draft, write the score in the treatment, and revise until it clears the bar.

| Area | Points | Full marks when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Hook (0–3 s)** | 15 | 1st word ≤ 0.5 s; lean → interjection → snapback; triple aligned; ≤ 12 words; nothing answered |
| **Promise & first value** | 8 | one promise by 8 s / 30 s; a concrete hit of value by 8 s / 60 s |
| **Causality (but/therefore)** | 12 | zero *and-then* joins; list items chained by consequence |
| **Loops & re-hooks** | 12 | ≥ 2 loops, nested ≤ 3, fed once, closed in reverse; re-hooks on schedule (§3.2/3.3) |
| **Stakes & escalation** | 10 | named protagonist with a want; ≥ 3 rungs; clear cost of failure |
| **Midpoint twist** | 6 | reframes the opening question; planted earlier |
| **Ending** | 8 | strongest payoff in the last 20%; callback to the first line/frame; no recap or goodbye; next-episode tease (YouTube) |
| **Line craft** | 12 | avg ≤ 12 words; short–short–long; concrete; numbers humanised; zero filler/clichés; punchlines last |
| **Emotional curve** | 9 | curiosity always on; a gag every ≤ 25 s / 60 s; one awe moment; high/low alternation |
| **Ear & accuracy** | 8 | read-aloud clean for Kokoro; symbols spelled out; every fact sourced and dated; nothing invented |

**Thresholds**
- **≥ 85: ship.** **75–84**: one targeted pass on the lowest two areas. **60–74**: restructure (usually causality, loops or the hook). **< 60**: new angle.
- **Auto-fail regardless of score**: hook fails checks 1–2 · any "and then" chain of 3+ beats · a recap or goodbye ending · an
  unpaid loop · an unverified fact or invented number · any line punching down at a people or place.
- Self-scoring is lenient. **Score twice**: once as the writer, once "as a bored 19-year-old on the bus with sound off". Take the lower score.

44   V: Iron-tipped stakes, hammered into the riverbed. Hidden at high tide.   (mechanism)

## 9. The writer's per-episode procedure (do it in this order)
1. Write the **title + thumbnail promise first** (MrBeast, Galloway: "plan your title and thumbnail before recording"). The script is downstream of the packaging.
2. Write the **ending first** (Pixar #7), then the hook that the ending answers.
3. Choose one structure (§3.7) and one stakes ladder (§5.2). List the loops: A (whole film), B, C, with open/feed/close timestamps.
4. Draft beats as **one line each** joined by BUT/THEREFORE. Fix the causality before writing any prose.
5. Write the prose to the timing table (§3.2/3.3) and the word budget (§3.1).
6. Run the detector (§6.1), rewrite the flagged lines (§6.2), read aloud, then score (§7) twice.
7. In the treatment, record `// hook:` (the triple), `// loops:` (A/B/C with times), `// ladder:`, `// twist:`, `// score:`.
