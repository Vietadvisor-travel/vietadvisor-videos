## 3. SCRIPT (A/V, hit words in bold; ≈ 12.1 min by pp_scriptlint --long, 2,035 words)
THESIS: Meta survived its data scandal because the scandal was the business working; one state counted the cost, and that bill is still unpaid.

## a0 · the same day
{tense} On Wednesday the twenty-first of October, twenty twenty-six, two documents about the same company came out in the same **day**.
In California, Meta reported sixty-four point six billion dollars of revenue in three **months**.
In Santa Fe, New Mexico, a judge ordered it to pay twenty-five billion **dollars**.
This is the story of how one personality quiz turned into eight years of bills, and how one state refused to **settle**.
Everyone says Meta was finally punished for Cambridge **Analytica**.
{wry} But Meta has not paid a single dollar of it. (J)
It has appealed. And the case was never only about Cambridge **Analytica**.
So how does a company survive the most famous data scandal of its time, and keep growing **anyway**?
Because the scandal was not a bug. It was the business working exactly as **designed**.
Keep an eye on the bill. It is the only thing in this story that never gets **paid**.

## a1 · the machine
It started at Harvard, on the fourth of February, two thousand **four**.
Mark Zuckerberg and Eduardo Saverin each put in a thousand **dollars**.
A year later, the company paid two hundred thousand dollars just for the name facebook dot **com**. (J)
In May twenty twelve, Facebook went public and raised about sixteen billion **dollars**.
A month earlier, it had bought Instagram for about one billion. Instagram had thirty million users and thirteen **employees**. (J)
Two years later, it paid about nineteen billion dollars for WhatsApp, which had four hundred and fifty million users and fifty-five **employees**.
The users never paid anything. The advertisers paid for the **users**.
And the more Facebook knew about each person, the more each advert was **worth**.
In those years, an app on Facebook could read more than the person who installed it. It could read their **friends**.
That same year, twenty twelve, Facebook had made a promise to the Federal Trade Commission. It would not mislead users about their **privacy**.
Remember that promise. It comes back with a price **tag**.
So how does one personality quiz end up costing twenty-five billion dollars? It starts with that one **permission**.

## a2 · the quiz
In twenty fourteen, a Cambridge University researcher named Aleksandr Kogan built a personality quiz called "This is your digital **life**."
About two hundred and seventy thousand people downloaded **it**.
Through their friend lists, the app reached far more people than that.
Facebook's own estimate, published by its chief technology officer in April twenty eighteen, was up to eighty-seven million **profiles**.
{wry} Other counts say fifty million, or sixty-five. Nobody outside the company has ever counted them **all**.
About eighty-two percent of those profiles belonged to people in the United **States**.
Kogan passed the data to a political consulting firm in London called Cambridge **Analytica**.
It was bankrolled by the billionaire Robert Mercer, and his daughter Rebekah sat on its **board**.
In twenty sixteen, the Trump campaign paid it five point nine million dollars, and the Cruz campaign five point eight million, election filings **show**.
Whether any of it changed a single vote is still **disputed**.
In twenty fifteen, Facebook found out where the data had **gone**.
It asked for the data to be deleted, and it accepted signed letters saying it **was**.
It did not check. And it did not tell the users. [BEAT]
That decision, made quietly in twenty fifteen, is the start of every bill in this **film**.

## a3 · the week it broke
{tense} For three years, nothing happened. Then, on Friday the sixteenth of March, twenty eighteen, Facebook suspended Cambridge Analytica from its platform, late in the **evening**.
The next morning, The Observer and The New York Times published within hours of each **other**.
Facebook had suspended Kogan and Wylie too, but the story was already **out**.
Their source was Christopher Wylie, a former director of research at Cambridge **Analytica**.
Two days later, Channel 4 News aired undercover footage of the firm's chief executive, Alexander **Nix**.
He talked about bribery stings, honey traps and front companies as possible **services**.
His board suspended him the next **day**. The same day, Britain's data regulator asked a court for a warrant to search the firm's London **offices**.
Mark Zuckerberg said nothing for five **days**.
Then, on the twenty-first of March, he posted a **statement**.
In April, he sat in front of the United States **Senate**, and the next day in front of the **House**.
@zuck: It was my mistake, and I'm sorry. I started Facebook, I run it, and I'm responsible for what happens here.
He also confirmed that his own data had been swept up with everyone **else's**. (J)
And he promised to grow Facebook's security staff to twenty thousand people by the end of the **year**.
On the second of May, Cambridge Analytica announced it was ceasing all **operations**.
It blamed a "siege of media coverage" that had driven away almost all of its **customers**.
The firm that used the data was gone in about six **weeks**.
Two years later, Nix accepted a seven-year ban on running British **companies**.
But the company that gave the data away was just getting **started**.

## a4 · the bills
{tense} So what does a scandal like that actually cost a company like this?
{grave} The first bill came from the stock **market**.
On the twenty-sixth of July, twenty eighteen, Facebook shares fell nineteen percent in a single **day**.
About one hundred and nineteen billion dollars of market value disappeared, the largest one-day loss for any US company at the **time**.
In October, Britain's data regulator fined it five hundred thousand **pounds**.
It found that at least one million British users had been put at **risk**.
That was the legal maximum. One tech news site worked out it was about seventeen minutes of Facebook's **profit**. (J)
In February twenty nineteen, a committee of the British Parliament called companies like Facebook "digital **gangsters**."
Zuckerberg had refused its invitations to give evidence three **times**. (J)
Then, on the twenty-fourth of July, twenty nineteen, the Federal Trade Commission announced a five billion dollar **penalty**.
It was the largest privacy penalty ever imposed at the time. But it was about nine percent of Facebook's revenue for one **year**.
The order also put a new privacy committee on Facebook's board, and made Zuckerberg personally sign off on privacy every **quarter**.
The same day, the SEC added one hundred million dollars, because Facebook had told investors that user data "may be" misused, when it already **knew**.
It had known for about two **years**. (J)
In twenty twenty-two, Meta agreed to pay seven hundred and twenty-five million dollars to settle a class action brought by **users**.
Shared among up to eighty-seven million people, that was a few dollars **each**.
In other privacy cases, Texas got one point four billion **dollars**.
And Ireland's regulator fined it one point two billion **euros**.
In twenty twenty-five, shareholders who had sued Zuckerberg and other executives for eight billion dollars settled in the middle of the trial, on terms nobody **disclosed**.
Every one of these was a record. But every one of them was **survivable**.
In twenty twenty-one, Facebook renamed itself **Meta**.
By twenty twenty-five, its yearly revenue had passed two hundred billion **dollars**.

## a5 · the holdout
{tense} One state did not settle.
In twenty twenty-one, New Mexico sued Facebook under its consumer protection **law**.
The attorney general who filed it was Hector Balderas. His successor, Raul Torrez, took the case **over**.
In August twenty twenty-six, a group of other states signed a settlement with Meta in **Oakland**.
New Mexico refused to sign it. It took its case to a **jury**.
One warning, because the headlines mix them up. New Mexico has a second, separate case against Meta, about children. This film is about the first **one**.
But the case was bigger than one quiz **app**.
It was about twenty-nine public statements Meta and its leaders had made, about privacy, hate speech, misinformation and **moderation**.
{grave} On Friday the twenty-fifth of September, twenty twenty-six, a jury in Santa Fe gave its **verdict**.
Twenty-six of those twenty-nine statements were **misleading**.
And then the jury did something no headline explained well. It **counted**.

## a6 · the arithmetic
New Mexico's law sets a penalty for every single **violation**.
So the question became: how many violations is one misleading **statement**?
The answer used in court was each statement, multiplied by everyone in New Mexico who could have seen **it**.
Not everyone who did see it. Everyone who **could**. (J)
The jury's total came to forty-three million, eight hundred and ninety-nine thousand, seven hundred and twenty-five **violations**.
Five of those were for something the law treats as worse than misleading: unconscionable **practices**.
That is not forty-three million victims. It is a counting **method**, and Meta's appeal will attack exactly that.
At five thousand dollars a violation, the law allowed two hundred and nineteen point five billion **dollars**.
At the penalty hearing on the first of October, the state's lawyer asked for thirty-five to forty **billion**.
Meta asked the court to stop at three point four five **billion**.
So the judge had a range, from about three and a half billion to two hundred and nineteen **billion**.
Three weeks later, Judge Francis Mathew signed a twenty-page **judgment**.
Judge Mathew wrote that Facebook's wrongdoing "goes to the heart of Facebook's core business **practices**."
Then the judge cut the penalty to twenty-five billion dollars. The law allowed almost nine times **more**.
Interest runs at fifteen percent a year until it is paid. More orders could still **come**.
{wry} And the money goes to the State of New Mexico. Not to a single **user**. (J)

## a7 · the bill and the business
{tense} Meta said it would appeal, and its chief legal officer said he was confident it would **win**.
Meta's position is simple. It says it does not sell user **data**.
And it says the state never proved that anyone was actually **misled**.
In its quarterly filing, Meta set money aside for the penalty, without saying **how much**.
So how big is twenty-five billion dollars for this **company**?
It is about thirty-nine percent of one quarter's **revenue**.
It is about thirty percent of the eighty-two billion dollars Meta holds in cash and **securities**.
Painful. But not **fatal**. In the same quarter, its revenue grew twenty-six percent, and three point six four billion people used its apps every **day**.
The real pressure in that same quarter came from somewhere **else**.
Meta booked a nine point eight nine billion dollar charge for a separate settlement over teenagers' mental **health**.
Its operating income fell thirty-seven **percent**.
It spent almost thirty-five billion dollars in three months, mostly on data centres for artificial **intelligence**.
So its free cash flow turned negative, minus two point one two billion **dollars**.
It plans to sell ninety-seven and a half billion dollars of new stock to pay for the **build-out**.
On the thirtieth of October, the ratings agency S and P moved Meta's outlook to **negative**.
{wry} Meta replied that it "respectfully" **disagreed**. (J)
So the company that can absorb a twenty-five billion dollar penalty is, at the same time, selling new shares to pay for its own **future**.

## a8 · the lesson
{warm} So here is the lesson. And it goes back to that promise from twenty **twelve**.
Meta did not fall when the scandal broke, because the scandal was the business working as **designed**.
The quiz used a permission Facebook gave every app. The data was valuable because Facebook made it **valuable**.
The fines were records, and the company paid them out of a growing **pile**. (J)
What changed in twenty twenty-six was not the scandal. It was the **arithmetic**.
One state refused to settle, and counted the cost statement by **statement**.
{awe} The bill is ordered. It is not **paid**.
If the appeal fails, New Mexico will have shown that a broken promise can be priced one statement at a time, at a scale that even Meta has to **notice**.
If it succeeds, then in the end the biggest bill in this story will stay exactly what it is today, a number on paper that nobody has ever paid, because the arithmetic behind it did not survive the **appeal**.
So tell us what you think: should a company pay for every misleading thing it tells its users, or only for what someone can prove it **did**?
