## a0 COLD
On Saturday, the eleventh of June, 2016, a woman in a red **gown** walked onto the stage at Wembley Arena.
Thousands of her investors filled the **arena**.
She promised them that in two years, nobody would speak about **Bitcoin** anymore.
She was selling investors the coin that would kill it. [BEAT]
That coin did not **exist**.
This is the story of how a product that never existed raised more than four **billion** dollars.
No blockchain. No mining. No market. Just a **database** her own company controlled.
But that's not the whole story. Two years before Wembley, she had already written the **ending**.
An email from August 2014, listing her exit **strategy**.
Option one: "Take the money and run and blame someone else for this."
She did all **three**. [BEAT] Almost to the word.
So who paid for it? Three and a half million investors, and the money is still **missing**.

## a1 THE PLAN
Ruja Ignatova was born in **Ruse**, Bulgaria, in 1980, and grew up in Germany.
On paper, the founder was the most **credible** person in any room.
A real law **doctorate** from the University of Konstanz.
A job at **McKinsey**.
Her marketing added **Oxford**. [BEAT] Her Oxford degree was a master's. (J)
What the marketing left out: in 2012, a German court convicted her of **fraud**.
She and her father had stripped a foundry until it went **bankrupt**. Her sentence: fourteen months, suspended.
Then, in 2013, she tried a first crypto scheme, **BigCoin**, alongside a Swedish salesman named Sebastian Greenwood.
In June 2014 she sent Greenwood an email pitching a bigger **deal**.
In her words: "you as the magic sales machine, and me as someone who really can work with numbers…"
"…like MLM meets bitch of wall **street**." (J)
In their private emails, the two founders even had a nickname for their product: "trashy **coin**." (J)
Two months later came the exit **email**.
So before a single coin was sold, the plan had three **steps**: take, run, blame.

## a2 TAKE THE MONEY
Step one. Here's the trick: investors never actually bought a **coin**.
They bought an education **package**: course notes that a US lawsuit says were copied from free sources like Wikipedia. (J)
Package prices started at one hundred euros and went up to **118,000**.
Each package came with tokens, and you "**mined**" the tokens into coins.
Mining meant a **timer**. When it ran out, a number appeared in your account. (J)
And the price of that number? The office in Sofia **typed** it in.
Fifty euro cents at launch. Twenty-nine ninety-five by **2019**.
Yet the coin never traded on a single public **market**.
Then came the **splits**: every few months, your tokens doubled overnight.
But each time, the "mining difficulty" quietly **rose**: from 44 tokens per coin to 359. (J)
So on screen, your balance only ever **grew**.
The company promised 120 billion coins. Bitcoin will only ever have twenty-one **million**.
And every buyer was also a **salesman**.
You got paid on your recruits, and on your recruits' **recruits**.
At the very top, Greenwood took five percent of all sales worldwide: over three hundred million **dollars**.
But your own bonus? Sixty percent cash. Forty percent locked inside the **system**. (J)
And even the cash was hard to get **out**.
The only exchange was OneCoin's own, with daily selling limits, and it kept going **offline**.
Which meant the money flowed one **way**: in.
OneCoin's own files, later obtained by US prosecutors, cover late 2014 to late **2016**.
Sales: four point zero three seven billion **euros**.
Booked as profit: two point seven three five **billion**.
That's two of every three euros, because the product cost almost nothing to **make**.
For scale: at the end of 2016, all the Bitcoin in the world was worth about fifteen and a half **billion** dollars.
OneCoin had already raised more than a **quarter** of that. For rows in a database.
In June 2016, at the height of the money, she took the stage at **Wembley**.
And that September, Greenwood sent her brother a message about the members: "These ppl are **idiots**." (J)
Her brother's message back: "the network would not work with intelligent **people**." (J)

## a3 THE CRACK
But a fake blockchain has one weakness: one day, you need a real **programmer**.
In October 2016, OneCoin launched a brand new **blockchain**, and doubled every member's balance.
That same season, a Norwegian developer named Bjørn **Bjercke** took a recruiter's phone call.
The job: build a blockchain for a cryptocurrency **company**.
"Well, first of all, they need a blockchain," the recruiter said. "They don't have a blockchain **today**."
Bjercke said no. Then he started **talking**.
By then, regulators in Norway, Germany and Britain had already moved against **it**.
In January 2017, OneCoin's internal exchange shut down for **good**.
And still, the selling didn't **stop**.
Because the sales team was the victims themselves, and doubting meant calling your own family a **fool**.
So who could stop a scheme that its own investors **protected**?
It wasn't a regulator. [BEAT] It was her **boyfriend**.
Gilbert **Armenta** moved money for OneCoin.
But from 2017, he was secretly cooperating with the **FBI**.
Prosecutors later called his help "**extraordinary**": it led to charges against at least fourteen people.
She was afraid someone close to her would give her **up**.
She was right.

## a4 AND RUN
Step two. On Saturday, the seventh of October, 2017, OneCoin opened a big event in **Lisbon**.
Its founder never **showed**.
Five days later, the United States issued a **warrant** for her arrest.
She told her brother she was very **tired**.
She had what he called a "big **passport**", and he bought her tickets to Vienna and to Athens.
On Wednesday, the twenty-fifth of October, 2017, she boarded a **Ryanair** plane from Sofia to Athens.
One bag. One **bodyguard**. [BEAT]
She landed in **Athens**.
And that is the last confirmed sighting of Ruja Ignatova. {hold}
She never contacted her brother **again**.
But the company didn't even **pause**.
Her brother, a former forklift driver, ran it for sixteen more **months**. (J)
Members kept buying coins into **2019**.
Take the money: done. Run: **done**.
One step left: blame someone **else**.

## a5 BLAME SOMEONE ELSE
The blame arrived **fast**.
In July 2018, Greenwood was arrested on the Thai island of Koh **Samui**.
In March 2019, her brother's plane landed in Los Angeles, and he was **arrested** on arrival.
New York's top federal prosecutor called OneCoin "a pyramid scheme based on smoke and mirrors more than zeroes and **ones**." (J)
Then came the man who washed the money: Mark Scott, a partner at a major law **firm**.
His fake investment funds moved about four hundred million **dollars**.
His fee: more than fifty **million**.
His defence: he didn't know it was a **scam**.
The jury took about four **hours**. (J)
So line up the **sentences**.
Greenwood, the salesman: twenty **years**.
Scott, the lawyer: ten **years**.
Armenta, the boyfriend who flipped, then broke his own deal: five **years**.
Irina Dilkinska, head of legal and **compliance**: four years. (J)
Konstantin, who testified about his sister's escape: time served, and **free**.
And Ruja? Charged on five counts, never **tried**. In law, she is presumed innocent.
Then even her escape got a **sequel**.
Frank Schneider, a former Luxembourg spy and OneCoin's security adviser, was arrested in France in **2021**.
Under house arrest, with a GPS tag on his ankle, he vanished in May **2023**.
He is still a **fugitive**.

## a6 THE BILL
So where is **she**?
In 2022, the FBI put her on its Ten Most Wanted list, the only **woman** on it at the time.
In 2024, the United States raised the reward to five million **dollars**.
A leaked Bulgarian police memo claims she was killed in **2018**.
The document is real. But the claim inside it is second-hand, and **unverified**.
So the FBI still hunts her as if she's **alive**.
Nobody has confirmed seeing her since **Athens**.
What we can confirm is the **bill**.
In Uganda, the BBC found farmers who had sold land and **livestock** to buy in.
In Germany, investigators traced the money of 17,552 victims through a single payment **processor**.
In Glasgow, Jen McAdam had brought in friends and family.
Together, she said, they had "thrown a quarter of a million euros **away**."
In 2026, the US opened a fund for victims: just over forty million **dollars**.
Against more than four billion lost, that's about one **cent** on the dollar. [BEAT]
Here's the real reason it worked: the fraud never needed smarter victims. It needed one person to open the **database**.
Nobody could, because the "blockchain" was **private**, the salesmen were family, and the money was everywhere.
Take the money. Run. Blame someone else. She wrote it in **2014**.
The coin never existed. The exit strategy **did**.
