# RESEARCH: OneCoin / Ruja Ignatova (slug: onecoin)

Episode 4 in the 30-day launch schedule (publish 3 November 2026). Schedule title: "The Crypto Queen Sold a Coin That Never Existed".

Confidence tags: high / medium / low. Every fact carries its source. Where sources conflict, the conflict is kept, never blended.

## A. Facts, ranked by surprise

1. The exit was written down before the launch. In an email to co-founder Karl Sebastian Greenwood on 9 August 2014, Ignatova listed OneCoin's "exit strategy" option one as: "Take the money and run and blame someone else for this . . . ." Source: DOJ, Greenwood guilty plea release, justice.gov. Confidence: high.
2. In their own 2014 emails, Ignatova and Greenwood privately called the product a "trashy coin" while marketing it as the future of money. Source: DOJ. Confidence: high.
3. In October 2016 OneCoin announced a "new blockchain" and doubled every member's coin balance overnight. In the same period the company tried to recruit Norwegian developer Bjorn Bjercke to build a blockchain. The recruiter told him: "Well, first of all, they need a blockchain. They don't have a blockchain today." He refused and later talked to the BBC and investigators. Source: Bjercke's account to the BBC. Confidence: high.
4. OneCoin's own internal records, obtained by US prosecutors: between Q4 2014 and Q4 2016 the scheme took EUR 4.037 billion in sales revenue and booked EUR 2.735 billion in "profits". Roughly two of every three euros taken in was profit, because the product cost almost nothing to fabricate. Source: DOJ/IRS releases. Confidence: high.
5. There was no blockchain and no mining. At Greenwood's sentencing the DOJ stated OneCoin lacked a public and verifiable blockchain, had no mining, and did not even have as many coins on its private ledger as it had sold. Balances were rows in a company-controlled SQL database. Source: DOJ SDNY, September 2023. Confidence: high.
6. On 25 October 2017 Ignatova boarded a Ryanair flight from Sofia to Athens with little more than a bag and one security guard, and was never publicly seen again. She never contacted her brother again. Source: FBI; Konstantin Ignatov's trial testimony as reported by the BBC/WSJ. Confidence: high.
7. Her boyfriend, Gilbert Armenta, was secretly cooperating with the FBI from 2017. Prosecutors called his cooperation "extraordinary": it helped indict at least 14 defendants, including both co-founders. He then breached his own deal (sold a jet he had agreed to surrender, stole a $5 million check, gambled with victim funds) and was sentenced to 5 years in February 2023. Source: SDNY court documents and Bloomberg Law reporting. Confidence: high on the cooperation and sentence, medium on the surveillance detail reported in filings.
8. Greenwood, the "global master distributor", took a 5 percent commission on all OneCoin sales worldwide: over $300 million personally, roughly EUR 20 million a month at the peak. Source: DOJ/IRS. Confidence: high.
9. Mark Scott, a former equity partner at law firm Locke Lord, built the "Fenero Funds", fake private-equity structures in the BVI and Cayman Islands, and laundered approximately $400 million of OneCoin proceeds through them. He was paid over $50 million in fees. Convicted November 2019; sentenced to 10 years plus $392.94 million forfeiture in January 2024. Source: DOJ. Confidence: high.
10. The coin's price was a number the company typed in. The SDNY indictment records it rising from EUR 0.50 per coin at launch to about EUR 29.95 by January 2019 (roughly 60x on paper). It never traded on any public exchange. Source: SDNY indictment quoted in DOJ releases. Confidence: high.
11. The "split" mechanism periodically doubled members' token balances while the "mining difficulty" quietly climbed from 44 to 359 tokens per coin across six splits, clawing back much of the free doubling. Sources: OneCoin's own compensation decks and member calculation sheets. Confidence: medium (the scheme's own marketing artifacts, which is their evidentiary value).
12. Bonuses were paid 60 percent to a "cash account" and 40 percent to a mandatory "trading account" usable only inside the system. A UK law-firm letter for victims records that withdrawing even the cash side was "great difficulty... or impossible". Sources: OneCoin compensation decks; Berryman Lace Mawer letter. Confidence: high.
13. The only cash-out route was the company's own internal exchange, never listed anywhere public, with daily selling limits tied to the size of the package you had bought. In the run-up to its shutdown OneCoin denied most withdrawal requests; in January 2017 the exchange closed entirely. Sources: contemporaneous accounts; Investopedia/AwesomeFinTech explainer. Confidence: high on structure, medium on per-tier caps.
14. After Ruja vanished, her brother Konstantin ran OneCoin for about 16 more months. Arrested at Los Angeles airport on 6 March 2019, he pleaded guilty, testified for the prosecution at Scott's trial (including a detailed account of his sister's flight), and in March 2024 was sentenced to 34 months, time served, and released. Source: DOJ/BBC. Confidence: high.
15. The disappearing trick repeated. Frank Schneider, a former deputy director of operations of Luxembourg's intelligence service and OneCoin's security adviser, was arrested in France in April 2021, fought extradition from house arrest, then cut his ankle monitor and vanished in May 2023, days after his extradition order was signed. Source: BBC; French court reporting. Confidence: high on the escape.

The reward path: FBI $100,000 (2022), raised to $250,000 (2023), then superseded by a US State Department Transnational Organized Crime reward of up to $5 million on 26 June 2024. ## F. The three decision moments

1. The coronation she did not have to give: Wembley, 11 June 2016. By mid-2016 regulators were circling and the "cryptocurrency" was a database she controlled. Instead of going quiet, Ignatova rented Wembley Arena, walked out in a ball gown to "Girl on Fire", and personally promised thousands of people that Bitcoin would be forgotten in two years. The fraud's fatal flaw, her own face, voice and promises on film forever, was created in one evening. Every prosecutor and documentary since opens with this footage (the FBI's public-domain cut is in the pack).
2. The outsider who heard the truth: the Bjercke call, October 2016. Weeks after announcing a shiny "new blockchain", OneCoin tried to hire a real blockchain developer to build one, and the recruiter said the quiet part aloud: "They don't have a blockchain today." Bjercke's decision to refuse, and then to talk, converted suspicion into proof. It is the moment the lie was confessed to someone outside the family, on the record.
3. The empty chair in Lisbon, and the one-way ticket: October 2017. With Armenta secretly flipped and a US warrant days away, Ignatova first ghosted her own event in Lisbon (7 October), then, over the following 18 days, converted a global empire into a single Ryanair seat: Sofia to Athens, 25 October, one bag, one bodyguard. She never contacted her brother again. It remains the cleanest vanishing in modern financial crime history.
Runner-up beats: Armenta's 2017 choice to cooperate in secret while inside her life and business; Konstantin's 2019 choice to testify against his own sister's company in open court; Schneider's 2023 ankle-tag escape, which rhymes with hers.

## G. The mechanism (how it actually worked)

1. Nothing underneath. No blockchain, no mining, no market. Coins were balances in a company SQL database; the company set the price (EUR 0.50 rising to EUR 29.95), minted supply at will (October 2016: everyone's balances doubled by announcement), and per the DOJ sold more coins than its own ledger even pretended to hold.
2. The product was a story sold in packages. Members bought OneAcademy "education" bundles (EUR 100 to 118,000) that came with tokens; tokens were "submitted to mining", which started a timer after which a number appeared in the member's back office. The education was largely plagiarized; the tokens were the point.
3. The split illusion. Periodic "splits" doubled tokens while "mining difficulty" climbed from 44 to 359 tokens per coin, clawing back the gift. Balances grew on screen; value did not.
4. The MLM engine. Direct bonus 10 percent, network bonus 10 percent of the weaker binary leg, matching bonuses across four generations (up to 10/10/20/25 percent at the top tier), a start-up bonus for fast recruiters. At the apex, Greenwood took 5 percent of all global sales. Recruitment was the product distribution and the growth engine in one.
5. The exits were designed shut. Bonuses landed 60 percent cash account / 40 percent locked trading account. The only exchange was internal, never public, with daily sell limits tied to package size and orders needing an internal buyer; it kept going offline and closed for good in January 2017. The replacement, DealShaker (2017), was a coupon marketplace where substantial goods still required a large cash portion: not an exit.
6. The wash. Proceeds were moved by professionals: Scott's Fenero Funds (about $400 million) through BVI/Cayman structures and complicit banks; Armenta (about $300 million); Dilkinska's Cayman route (over $110 million). The money surfaced as London penthouses, Bulgarian mansions, a yacht, Dubai property and luxury spending.

## H. Counterfactual

The fraud did not need better victims; it needed one inspection. Any regulator, bank or journalist who could have compelled a look at the "blockchain" in 2015 would have found a database, and the story ends two billion euros earlier. Three things prevented that look: the asset was deliberately unauditable (a "private blockchain" whose privacy was the excuse); the sales force was the victims themselves, so suspicion read as disloyalty inside thousands of family networks; and jurisdiction was smeared across Sofia, Dubai, Belize and 175 countries of buyers, so every national regulator saw only a slice. The first person outside the family who was accidentally told the truth, a job candidate in 2016, ended it intellectually; it took the US flipping the founder's boyfriend to end it legally, and by then she was already gone.

## I. Comparisons

- BitConnect (DOJ: about $2.4 billion taken): OneCoin took roughly 1.7 times more, with no coin, no exchange listing and no trading at all.
- Madoff: about $19.5 billion of real principal over decades, mostly institutional and feeder-fund money; OneCoin extracted over $4 billion of retail cash in about two years. Recovery: Madoff victims have recovered the large majority of principal; OneCoin victims face about one percent ($40 million against $4 billion plus).
- Bitcoin itself: at the end of 2016 all Bitcoin in existence was worth about $15.5 billion; OneCoin's cash take by then exceeded a quarter of that, for database entries.
- Within the case: Greenwood's $300 million personal take and 20 years versus Konstantin's cooperation and time served; the spread shows exactly what the US bought with leniency: the insider account of her escape.

## J. Myths versus reality

- "OneCoin was a cryptocurrency that failed." No: per the DOJ there was never a cryptocurrency, no blockchain, no mining and no market price. It did not collapse like a bad coin; it was a sales scheme with a database front.
- "She is confirmed dead." No: the November 2018 killing story is a second-hand claim in a genuine police document, unverified by any police force; the FBI and German investigators proceed as if she is alive, and the US raised the reward to $5 million in 2024.
- "She is confirmed alive in Russia / Cape Town / Dubai." Also unproven: all post-2017 sightings are unconfirmed reporting. The honest position: no confirmed sighting since Athens airport, 25 October 2017.
- "The total was $15 billion (or $19.4 billion)." Unproven: the documented floor is the DOJ's $4 billion plus (internal books to end-2016). Higher figures are estimates by advocates or journalists; $19.4 billion is a corrupted copy of a Madoff figure and must never be used.
- "Her Oxford PhD." She held a genuine Konstanz PhD; the Oxford credential was a master's. The doctorate was real, the branding was inflated.
- "Her brother got away with it." He cooperated, testified and still served 34 months, and lives with the threats that followed; the sentencing spread across the cast (20 years to time served) maps exactly onto who flipped and when.
- "Konstantin faced 90 years." That was the statutory maximum across counts at his plea; his served sentence was 34 months. Both numbers are real; do not conflate them.

## K. Takeaways (for the thesis the director chooses)

- The product was belief with a login screen: a number that only rose, in an account you could look at but never leave. Every mechanic (splits, difficulty, the 60/40 bonus split, the internal exchange) existed to make the balance feel real while the exit stayed shut.
- The credentials were the camouflage: a real PhD, a McKinsey line and a stage big enough to make "Bitcoin killer" sound like analysis. The scheme's true technology was borrowed credibility, MLM trust networks and an unauditable asset.
- The ending inverted the org chart: the founder vanished, the brother flipped, the lawyer and the distributor took 10 and 20 years, and the victims' recovery is about one cent on the dollar. Not checking was distributed across regulators, banks, stages and 3.5 million buyers, and so was the cost.

Differentiation must come from the documented extras competitors underuse: the 2014 exit-strategy email, the split/difficulty mechanics, the Bjercke recruitment call, the 60/40 bonus trap, the German IMS processor (17,552 victims), the one-cent-on-the-dollar remission, and Schneider's mirrored escape.
- Alive-versus-dead discipline: the murder claim is low confidence and contested; the episode must present it as a claim with a paper trail, never as the answer. The FBI framing (alive, $5 million reward, 2024) is the current official line.
- Figures discipline: always window the EUR 4.037 billion (Q4 2014 to Q4 2016); never add China's figures to the DOJ's; never add the GBP 8.59 million to the EUR 10.1 million (same London asset pool); never use $19.4 billion; the EUR 29.95 price date is January 2019, not 2018.
- Portraits: no openly licensed photographs exist for Greenwood, Scott, Armenta, Schneider or Dilkinska (verified searches). The pack covers Ruja (three real images plus FBI footage), Bartlett, and places/documents in depth; the rest of the cast must be carried by art plates, documents and narration, or by licensed press photos the director sources separately.
- Legal framing: Ignatova is charged, not convicted. Keep one clear line to that effect (every serious competitor video carries it) and attribute allegations to prosecutors.
- Timing: publish is 3 November 2026, three days after episode 3. The DOJ remission process (2026) and the Guernsey recovery give the episode a genuine "where the money is now" ending that older treatments lack.

## Candidate angles (director chooses)

1. The coin that never existed (mechanism angle, matches the schedule title): the episode as an autopsy of a nothing: a database, a price typed in, splits that gave with one hand and took with the other. The horror is how little machinery $4 billion required.
2. Everyone fell but her (reckoning angle): the cast as a ladder of consequences, 20 years at the top of the sales machine, 10 for the lawyer, time served for the brother who talked, an empty chair for the founder. The manhunt is the spine; the sentences are the chapters.
3. One cent on the dollar (money-trail angle): follow the EUR 4 billion to penthouses, yachts and shell companies, and end in the 2026 remission queue where $40 million meets $4 billion of claims. The victims, not the queen, hold the camera.
