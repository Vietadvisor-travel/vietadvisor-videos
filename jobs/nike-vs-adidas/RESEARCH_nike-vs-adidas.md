# RESEARCH: Nike vs Adidas, the empire that dismantled itself and the rival that picked up the profit

Slug: nike-vs-adidas. Keyword: nike vs adidas. Research only. No script, no render.
Starting points tested, not assumed: schedule thesis "Nike was not beaten by Adidas; Nike dismantled its own culture machine in 24 months, and Adidas just picked up the pieces." Schedule hook: "On October 2, 2026, Nike's stock hit a 13-year low." Schedule title: "Nike Is in Crisis: The Empire Dismantled Itself in 24 Months".

Evidence tags used in this file. C means confirmed by a primary document or by two independent reputable sources. R means reported by one reputable outlet or a secondary summary, not primary verified. A means analyst or third-party estimate. D means disputed, contested, or legend. D items are never stated as fact in the script. UNVERIFIED means no usable source was found in this pass.

## A. Topic framing

The schedule thesis survives testing in softened form, and fails in its hardest form. What the sources support:

1. Nike's damage was substantially self-inflicted and documented: the 2020 to 2024 direct-to-consumer pivot cut wholesale partners, reorganized the company away from sport categories, and produced a documented innovation complaint trail (Bloomberg, September 2024; NPR, October 2024). The channel reversal is measurable: Nike Brand Direct fell from about 44 percent in FY24 to 39 percent in FY26 while wholesale became the growing channel again.
2. Adidas did not beat Nike by taking Nike's shoe crown. In the very quarters of the 2026 divergence, Adidas footwear grew only 1 percent currency-neutral in Q2 2026 and was flat currency-neutral in Q3 2026. Adidas won on apparel (up 35 percent in Q2, up 19 percent in Q3), on Performance (up 39 percent in Q2, up 14 percent in Q3), on direct-to-consumer, and on profit (a record EUR 1,008M operating profit in Q3 2026). The honest verb is not "beat" but "out-earned and out-grew while Nike shrank".
3. The "24 months" in the schedule title is the weakest phrase. The dismantling took more than 24 months: the direct pivot ran 2020 to 2024, and the bill arrived across FY25 (revenue down 10 percent) and FY26 into Q1 FY27. If the script keeps "24 months", it must define the window, for example October 2024 to October 2026, Hill's first two years failing to turn the company before the October 2026 low. Otherwise use "four years" for the pivot and "two years" for the failed turnaround.
4. The October 2026 hook is VERIFIED and is the strongest cold open in the file: October 2, 2026, intraday low USD 31.97, close USD 33.87, the lowest since September 2013.

Recommended thesis for the script, rewritten from evidence: "Nike spent four years rebuilding itself around selling direct and stopped making the shoes people lined up for. Adidas spent three years selling the shoes it already had in its archive. In October 2026 the market priced both decisions on the same week."

F06 [C] Phil Knight, a former University of Oregon middle-distance runner, and Bill Bowerman, his former track coach, formed Blue Ribbon Sports in 1964. Knight had toured the Onitsuka factory in Japan in 1962 and made a deal to distribute the Onitsuka Tiger in the United States. Source: Britannica.
F07 [C] Blue Ribbon split with Onitsuka in 1971 and took the Nike name. Carolyn Davidson, a Portland State graphic design student, designed the Swoosh for USD 35 at USD 2 per hour for 17.5 hours; it was trademarked June 18, 1971. F08 [C] The hand-built Moon Shoe, its waffle sole made by pouring rubber into a waffle iron, and the Cortez debuted at the 1972 US Olympic Trials in Eugene. The Moon Shoe was the first hand-built Nike trial shoe; the Cortez was the first commercial Nike shoe. The Waffle Trainer followed commercially in 1974. Nike went public in December 1980. Source: Nike official Bowerman history; Britannica.
F09 [C] In 1984 Nike signed rookie Michael Jordan to a five-year, USD 2.5M contract, USD 500,000 per year, with a signature line and a share of revenue or royalties rather than a flat fee only. Source: Nike official banned history (updated October 21, 2025); ESPN (2016).

## G. The 3 decision moments

Moment 1: Bet the company on a rookie, 1984, Beaverton. Nike, struggling in basketball against Adidas and Converse, gives a rookie a signature line and a revenue share instead of a flat fee. Jordan wanted Adidas; Adidas did not match the offer. First-year sales reported above USD 100M against internal projections reported in the low single millions. This is the decision that made Nike a culture company, and the standard every later Nike CEO is judged against.
Moment 2: Cut the stores that made you, June 2020, Beaverton. Donahoe launches Consumer Direct Acceleration, exits a list of wholesale accounts, and reorganizes around consumer segments instead of sports. The margin logic is real (Direct at about 44 percent of Brand channels by FY24) and the cultural cost is documented (Bloomberg, September 2024; NPR, October 2024). The reversal under Hill proves the decision's cost better than any critic: wholesale is the growing channel again by FY26, while Direct falls to 39 percent.
Moment 3: Sell the shoes you already own, 2023, Herzogenaurach. Gulden inherits a company forecasting a EUR 700M operating loss if Yeezy stock is written off, and instead scales the archive: Samba, Gazelle, Spezial, Campus. No new invention saves Adidas. The rescue is distribution of an old shoe to a new customer (the young female consumer, in Gulden's own 2025 Annual Report words) plus the disciplined selloff of Yeezy stock at about EUR 1.4B. The contrast with Moment 2 is the episode's spine: Nike abandoned its distribution to chase margin; Adidas used its archive to rebuild distribution.

## H. Mechanism

Nike's mechanism is a channel story with a product consequence. Selling direct captures more margin per pair and more customer data, but it removes the shelf where casual buyers discover shoes, it concentrates the business on a few retro franchises (Air Force 1, Dunk, Jordan Retro) that can be sold through an app, and it starves the wholesale partners who used to do Nike's local marketing. When retro fatigue arrived, there was no broad shelf to catch the fall, and the innovation pipeline had a gap that On, Hoka, and Asics filled in premium running (secondary analysis; the running-share claim needs a primary market-data source before it is stated as fact on screen). Adidas's mechanism is the mirror: an archive shoe with low fashion risk, scaled supply, and full-price discipline. Terrace demand was already there in 2023; Gulden's contribution was scaling it and then refusing to chase discount volume (Q3 2026 footwear flat on conservative wholesale sell-in to protect full-price positioning is the proof). The 2026 divergence is therefore not one company beating the other at the same game. Nike lost the shelf and the hype cycle; Adidas won the margin and the quarter on apparel and Performance while its own footwear stood still.

## I. Counterfactual

If Adidas had matched Nike's 1984 Jordan offer, Nike's basketball business may never have existed in its modern form, and Jordan Brand (about USD 7B in FY24 before its FY25 decline, per secondary summaries of Nike filings) would have been an Adidas division. If Nike had kept its wholesale shelf and treated Direct as an addition rather than a replacement in 2020, the retro fatigue of 2025 to 2026 would have had a broader base to land on, and the FY25 revenue fall of 10 percent might have been a dip rather than a reset. If Adidas had written off the Yeezy stock in early 2023 as its own forecast allowed (a EUR 700M operating loss year), the 2023 to 2025 V shape (EUR 268M to EUR 1,337M to EUR 2,056M operating profit) would have started from a deeper hole and the charity set-aside of EUR 260M would not exist.

## K. Myths and corrections

MYTH 1: The USD 5,000-a-game fine. FALSE in its popular form. Jordan wore the Air Ship, not the Air Jordan 1, in the flagged October 18, 1984, preseason game. ESPN (2016) found no evidence Nike ever paid a fine. The black and red Air Jordan 1 appeared on court only in two preseason games and the 1985 Slam Dunk Contest per Nike's own 2025 history. Use the Granik letter, not the fine legend.
MYTH 2: The bomb shelter caused the Dassler split. UNVERIFIED anecdote. The documented picture is a cluster: denazification accusations, resentment over wartime roles, and wives who did not get along. Do not use the anecdote as the cause.
MYTH 3: Adidas lost USD 1.3B on Reebok (USD 3.8B paid minus USD 2.5B received). WRONG arithmetic. It ignores the Rockport, CCM Hockey, and Greg Norman divestments (EUR 0.4B), years of Reebok trading, impairments, carrying value, deferred and contingent consideration, and tax. The booked 2022 result was a pre-tax gain of EUR 522M on the carrying value sold.
MYTH 4: The Yeezy forecast loss was an actual loss. WRONG. The EUR 700M operating loss was a 2023 forecast IF inventory was not sold. Adidas sold the stock for about EUR 1.4B across 2023 and 2024, and 2023 operating profit came in at EUR 268M, almost EUR 1B better than initially expected.
MYTH 5: Nike's Q4 FY26 profit surge proves recovery. WRONG without the caveat. Q4 net income up 407 percent included about USD 986M of expected IEEPA tariff recovery. Operating recovery must be judged on Q1 FY27, which fell.
MYTH 6: The 2026 World Cup proved Nike's brand is back. WRONG as a commercial claim. Press reported 66M-plus YouTube views in under a week for "Rip the Script", but Nike's June to August 2026 quarter revenue fell 4 percent and Greater China fell 26 percent currency-neutral. Views are not sales.
MYTH 7: Adidas's revival is a Samba sales figure. UNVERIFIED as an Adidas number. Adidas never disclosed a Samba-only revenue line in sources retrieved. The usable numbers are Originals franchise growth and the Bernstein Terrace estimate of about EUR 1.5B for 2024, attributed to Bernstein.
MYTH 8: Nike's first billion, global number one in 1986. CONTESTED. Nike revenue first crossed USD 1B in FY86 (USD 1.069B) but dipped the next year; the sustained crossing was FY88. The claim that Nike passed Adidas globally in 1986 conflicts with a secondary analysis putting the worldwide sales overtake in 1995. Do not state 1986 as the global overtaking year.
MYTH 9: A single FY26 Nike severance total. UNVERIFIED. One source reports a March warning of about USD 300M expected charges mostly severance; another tracker reports the FY26 annual report settled at USD 385M with a USD 230M charge in fiscal Q3. Do not use one total on screen until the FY26 10-K is checked.
MYTH 10: An Adidas and Ye settlement in October 2024 with no money changing hands. UNVERIFIED in this pass (FashionLaw Journal and a Medium investigation; no Reuters settlement report and no Adidas settlement release retrieved). Keep as "reported, not independently verified here" or cut it.

## L. Takeaways

1. Distribution is strategy, not logistics. Nike's 2020 decision to replace wholesale with direct is the single most expensive sentence in this episode, and its reversal under Hill is the proof.
2. Archives beat inventions when the customer is tired. Adidas did not invent a new shoe to recover; it scaled old shoes to a new customer and protected full price while doing it.
3. Attention and revenue decoupled in 2026. The most-watched sports ad of the year belonged to the company whose quarter shrank. Any script that treats views as victory will mislead.
4. The family split never ended; it changed form. Herzogenaurach 1948 was a split over a factory. Beaverton 2020 and Herzogenaurach 2023 are a split over a business model: direct versus shelf, new versus archive.

## 3 candidate angles and 3 candidate hooks (Claude chooses)

ANGLE 1, mechanism, recommended: "The shelf is the strategy." Nike replaced wholesale with direct and lost discovery, hype, and local marketing; Adidas kept full-price discipline and scaled an archive shoe. The episode is decided by distribution, not by design talent. Strongest evidence: Direct 44 percent to 39 percent, wholesale growing again; Adidas Q3 footwear flat on deliberate conservative sell-in.
ANGLE 2, attention trap: "Nike won the World Cup and lost the quarter." ANGLE 3, family echo: "The split never ended." Two brothers split a factory in Herzogenaurach in 1948 over who controlled the work. Seventy-five years later, a Nike lifer tries to restore a culture from inside while a Puma man runs Adidas on an archive. Same town logic, new form: insider restoration versus archive revival.

HOOK 1, schedule hook, VERIFIED: "On October 2, 2026, Nike's stock hit an intraday low not seen in 13 years: USD 31.97. ## Top-3 surprise facts, ranked

HOOK CANDIDATE: Jordan wanted Adidas. In 1984 Adidas was the bigger company by revenue and the brand Jordan wanted, and it did not match Nike's offer. Nike's USD 2.5M five-year rookie deal produced first-year sales reported above USD 100M against internal projections reported in the low single millions.
