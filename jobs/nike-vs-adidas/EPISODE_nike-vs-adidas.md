## 3. SCRIPT
# nike-vs-adidas · script (A/V, narrator supertonic:M1)
THESIS: Nike took its own shoes off the shelf; Adidas sold the shoes it had.

## a0 COLD · the same week
{tense} On the second of October, twenty twenty-six, Nike's stock touched thirty-one dollars and ninety-seven **cents**.
An intraday low not seen in thirteen years. So how did the most famous brand in sport fall this **far**?
This is the story of how two brothers, one rookie and one very expensive decision got us **here**.
Three weeks after that low, Adidas reported the most profitable quarter in its **history**.
Everyone says Adidas finally beat **Nike**.
{wry} But that quarter, Adidas's shoe sales didn't grow at **all**. (J)
So that's not the real story. Nike took its own shoes off the **shelf**.
And Adidas sold the shoes it already **had**. [BEAT]
Remember the word shelf. It decides the **ending**.

## a1 BROTHERS · the split and the swoosh
It starts in a small Bavarian town, Herzogenaurach, in nineteen twenty-**four**.
Two brothers, Adi and Rudolf Dassler, register a shoe factory **together**.
In nineteen thirty-six, Jesse Owens wins four Olympic gold medals in their **spikes**.
Then, in nineteen forty-eight, the brothers **split**.
Rudolf founds the company that becomes Puma. Adi registers his own in nineteen forty-nine, with forty-seven employees and three **stripes**.
The town splits with them, one factory on each side of the **river**.
In nineteen sixty-four, in Oregon, a young runner, Phil Knight, and his coach, Bill Bowerman, start importing Japanese running **shoes**.
In nineteen seventy-one they become Nike. A design student draws the logo for thirty-five **dollars**. (J)
Bowerman makes a new sole by pouring rubber into a waffle **iron**.
So Adidas was the giant. Nike was the **upstart**.

## a2 THE BET · 1984
By nineteen eighty-four, Adidas was the bigger company, and the rookie Michael Jordan wanted **Adidas**.
But Adidas didn't match Nike's **offer**.
Nike gave him five years, two and a half million dollars, and his own **shoe**.
That October, Jordan wore a black and red Nike in a preseason game, and the league flagged **it**.
{wry} The legend says Nike paid a fine every game. ESPN found no evidence it ever paid **one**. (J)
It wasn't even the Air Jordan. It was a shoe called the Air **Ship**.
So Nike turned the warning into an ad: **banned**.
The Air Jordan went on sale in nineteen eighty-five, at sixty-five **dollars**.
By the end of that year, its sales had passed one hundred million **dollars**.
That was Nike's real formula: a shoe people lined up for, on shelves **everywhere**.
Remember that **shelf**.

## a3 THE SHELF · Nike 2020–2024
For thirty-five years, that formula worked. Then, in June twenty twenty, Nike's chief executive, John Donahoe, changed it. He called it Consumer Direct **Acceleration**.
The plan: sell through Nike's own app and stores, and cut out the **middlemen**. (J)
Nike walked away from Belk, Dillard's, Zappos and other **stores**.
By fiscal twenty twenty-four, direct sales were about forty-four percent of Nike brand **sales**.
The margin was higher. But it meant the shelf was **shrinking**.
And the app kept selling the same few classics: Dunks, Air Force Ones, Jordan **retros**.
{wry} A Bloomberg profile of Donahoe was titled "The Man Who Made Nike **Uncool**."
{grave} In fiscal twenty twenty-five, revenue fell ten **percent**.
{grave} Net income fell forty-four **percent**.
Donahoe left. Elliott Hill, who started at Nike as an intern in nineteen eighty-eight and retired in twenty twenty, came back as chief **executive**.
His first job: get Nike back on the **shelves**.

## a4 THE ARCHIVE · Adidas 2022–2025
But here's the part nobody saw coming. Adidas, meanwhile, had its own **disaster**.
In October twenty twenty-two, it ended its partnership with Ye, the rapper once known as Kanye **West**.
@adidas: adidas does not tolerate antisemitism and any other sort of hate speech.
It was left with a warehouse full of Yeezy **shoes**.
And a forecast: a seven-hundred-million-euro operating loss in twenty twenty-three, if it couldn't sell **them**.
The new chief executive, Bjorn Gulden, came from **Puma**.
@gulden: The numbers speak for themselves. We are currently not performing the way we should.
Then he did something deeply unglamorous. He sold what Adidas already **had**. (J)
The Samba. The Gazelle. The Spezial. Old shoes, made in far bigger **numbers**.
The Samba was named shoe of the year, the brand's first since twenty **fifteen**.
And instead of writing the Yeezys off, Adidas sold them, for about one point four billion **euros**.
It set aside two hundred and sixty million for **charity**.
Operating profit went from two hundred and sixty-eight million euros in twenty twenty-three to over two billion in twenty twenty-**five**.
Adidas had climbed back onto the **shelf**.

## a5 THE SAME WEEK · 2026
So who actually won twenty twenty-six? First came the World **Cup**.
Nike's six-minute film, Rip the Script, passed sixty-six million YouTube views in under a week, the press **reported**.
Adidas's ad stayed under seven **million**.
{wry} Nike won the attention. Then it reported the **quarter**. (J)
{grave} Revenue fell four percent. In Greater China, it fell twenty-**six**.
Nike told investors to expect revenue to fall again this fiscal **year**.
{tense} The next day, the stock hit its thirteen-year **low**.
Nike's chief executive, Elliott Hill, put it this **way**.
@hill: Our Nike performance business is not yet large enough to offset the pressure we are seeing in Nike sportswear, Jordan brand, and Greater China.
Three weeks later, Adidas made just over a billion euros of operating profit in a single quarter, the first time in its **history**.
About two hundred million euros of it was a one-off tariff **refund**.
And its footwear sales were flat. Adidas grew on clothing and sports **gear**.
{wry} So Adidas didn't beat Nike at shoes. Nike had stepped off the **shelf**.

## a6 THE LESSON · the shelf
Today, wholesale is Nike's growing channel again: sixty-one percent of brand sales in fiscal twenty twenty-**six**.
The company behind the world's most famous shoe forgot that people have to find it **first**.
And the company facing a seven-hundred-million-euro loss was saved by shoes from its own **archive**.
{warm} Here's the lesson. The shelf is the strategy. Where you sell is part of what you **sell**.
Nike shows investors its new plan on the seventeenth of **November**.
{awe} The question is whether a company can win back a shelf it walked away **from**.
So remember that shelf from nineteen eighty-five, because Nike built an empire on being everywhere and then decided it didn't need to be, while Adidas simply showed up wherever people **shop**.
If Nike gets its shoes back on those shelves, this was a bad two years, and if it doesn't, the brothers' old rival may be the one still **standing**.
Tell us in the comments: the new Nike, or the old **Samba**?
