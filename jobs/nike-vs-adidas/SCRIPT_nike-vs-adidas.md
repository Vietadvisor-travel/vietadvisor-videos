# nike-vs-adidas · script (A/V, narrator supertonic:M1)
THESIS: Nike took its own shoes off the shelf; Adidas sold the shoes it had.

## a0 · the same week
{tense} On the second of October, twenty twenty-six, Nike's stock touched thirty-one dollars and ninety-seven **cents**.
An intraday low not seen in thirteen years. So how did the most famous brand in sport fall this **far**?
This is the story of how two brothers, one rookie and one very expensive decision got us **here**.
Three weeks after that low, Adidas reported the most profitable quarter in its **history**.
Everyone says Adidas finally beat **Nike**.
{wry} But that quarter, Adidas's shoe sales didn't grow at **all**. (J)
So that's not the real story. Nike took its own shoes off the **shelf**.
And Adidas sold the shoes it already **had**. [BEAT]
Remember the word shelf. It decides the **ending**.

## a1 · the brothers
It starts in a small Bavarian town, Herzogenaurach, in nineteen twenty-**four**.
Two brothers, Adi and Rudolf Dassler, register a shoe factory **together**.
In nineteen thirty-six, At the Berlin Olympics of nineteen thirty-six, Jesse Owens wins four gold medals in their **spikes**.
Then, in nineteen forty-eight, the brothers **split**.
Nobody agrees on exactly why. The famous bomb-shelter story is **unproven**.
The records point to the war, to old grudges, and to two wives who did not get **along**.
Rudolf founds the company that becomes Puma. Adi registers his own in nineteen forty-nine, with forty-seven employees and three **stripes**.
The town splits with them, one factory on each side of the **river**.
Families, shops and social life split by **employer**.
In nineteen fifty-four, West Germany wins the World Cup final on a wet pitch, in Adidas boots with screw-in **studs**.
From nineteen seventy, Adidas makes the official World Cup **ball**.
So for decades, the biggest name in sport was **German**.

## a2 · the upstart
{tense} So how did a nobody from Oregon take that shelf? It starts with a **trip**.
In nineteen sixty-two, a young American runner named Phil Knight tours a shoe factory in **Japan**.
He makes a deal to sell its Onitsuka Tiger running shoes in the United **States**.
In nineteen sixty-four, he and his old college coach, Bill Bowerman, start Blue Ribbon **Sports**.
Knight had run for Bowerman at the University of **Oregon**.
In nineteen seventy-one they become Nike. A design student draws the logo for thirty-five **dollars**. (J)
It is trademarked that **June**.
That is two dollars an hour, for seventeen and a half **hours**. (J)
Twelve years later, Knight gives her a gold Swoosh ring and five hundred Nike **shares**.
Bowerman makes a new sole by pouring rubber into a waffle **iron**.
The Waffle Trainer goes on sale in nineteen seventy-**four**.
In nineteen seventy-two, the waffle-soled Moon Shoe and the Cortez make their debut at the US Olympic Trials in **Eugene**.
In December nineteen eighty, Nike goes **public**.
So Adidas was the giant. Nike was the **upstart**.

## a3 · the bet
By nineteen eighty-four, Adidas was the bigger company, and the rookie Michael Jordan wanted **Adidas**.
He had worn Converse **before**. (J)
But Adidas didn't match Nike's **offer**.
Nike gave him five years, two and a half million dollars, and his own **shoe**.
And a share of every pair sold, not just a flat **fee**.
That was five hundred thousand dollars a **year**.
On Thursday the eighteenth of October, nineteen eighty-four, Jordan wore a black and red Nike in a preseason game, and the league flagged **it**.
The rule said a shoe had to be at least fifty-one percent **white**.
{wry} The legend says Nike paid a fine every game. ESPN found no evidence it ever paid **one**. (J)
The black and red Air Jordan itself played just two preseason games.
Then came the nineteen eighty-five dunk **contest**.
It wasn't even the Air Jordan. It was a shoe called the Air **Ship**.
So Nike turned the warning into an ad: **banned**.
The Air Jordan went on sale in nineteen eighty-five, at sixty-five **dollars**.
By the end of that year, its sales had passed one hundred million **dollars**.
That was Nike's real formula: a shoe people lined up for, on shelves **everywhere**.
Remember that **shelf**.

## a4 · the empire
The formula kept working. In nineteen eighty-seven, Nike put a window in the sole: Air **Max**.
A year later came three words, written by its ad agency: Just Do **It**.
The first ad starred a runner named Walt **Stack**.
Adidas found its audience somewhere else. In nineteen eighty-six, Run-DMC released "My **Adidas**."
At Madison Square Garden, fans held up their Superstars, and Adidas signed the **band**.
Adidas calls it the birth of non-athletic promotion in sporting **goods**. (J)
Nike's real scare came from Reebok, which took the American lead in the late eighties on aerobics **shoes**.
But Nike took the lead back in nineteen eighty-**eight**.
In nineteen ninety-seven, Jordan got his own brand, with the Jumpman instead of the **Swoosh**.
Nike's yearly sales passed one billion dollars in the mid-eighties.
They reached six and a half billion by nineteen ninety-six, and ten billion by two thousand **three**.
So in two thousand six, Adidas bought Reebok for about three point eight billion dollars, to fight Nike in **America**.
It even dressed the NBA, until Nike took that contract over in twenty **seventeen**.
In twenty thirteen, Kanye West left Nike for Adidas. The first Yeezy Boost arrived in twenty **fifteen**.
In early twenty seventeen, Adidas grew faster than Nike in North America, on Boost, Superstar and Stan **Smith**.
By twenty seventeen, Adidas had climbed to about eleven percent of the American sneaker **market**.
Nike still had thirty-seven **percent**.
Nike didn't need to chase anyone. It was on every **shelf**.

## a5 · the shelf
For thirty-five years, that formula worked.
Then, in June twenty twenty, Nike's chief executive, John Donahoe, changed it.
He called it Consumer Direct **Acceleration**.
The plan: sell through Nike's own app and stores, and cut out the **middlemen**. (J)
Nike walked away from Belk, Dillard's, Zappos and other **stores**.
It had already left Amazon in twenty **nineteen**.
In fiscal twenty twenty, about three quarters of Nike brand sales still went through other people's **shops**.
By fiscal twenty twenty-four, direct sales were about forty-four **percent**.
The margin was higher. But it meant the shelf was **shrinking**.
And the app kept selling the same few classics: Dunks, Air Force Ones, Jordan **retros**.
In twenty twenty-four, NPR reported the complaint that Nike had lost its innovation **mojo**.
Analysts blamed the same shoes, dropped again and again in new **colours**.
{wry} A Bloomberg profile of Donahoe was titled "The Man Who Made Nike **Uncool**."
{grave} In fiscal twenty twenty-five, revenue fell ten **percent**.
{grave} Net income fell forty-four **percent**.
And fiscal twenty twenty-six was **flat**. (J)
Donahoe left. Elliott Hill came back as chief executive.
He had started at Nike as an intern in nineteen eighty-eight, and retired in twenty **twenty**.
His plan, Win Now, reorganised Nike around sports again and rebuilt its ties with the **stores**.
His first job: get Nike back on the **shelves**.

## a6 · the archive
But here's the part nobody saw coming. Adidas, meanwhile, had its own **disaster**.
In October twenty twenty-two, it ended its partnership with Ye, the rapper once known as Kanye **West**.
@adidas: adidas does not tolerate antisemitism and any other sort of hate speech.
It was left with a warehouse full of Yeezy **shoes**.
And a forecast: a seven-hundred-million-euro operating loss in twenty twenty-three, if it couldn't sell **them**.
The new chief executive, Bjorn Gulden, came from **Puma**, where he had been chief executive since twenty **thirteen**.
@gulden: The numbers speak for themselves. We are currently not performing the way we should.
Then he did something deeply unglamorous. He sold what Adidas already **had**. (J)
The Samba. The Gazelle. The Spezial. Old shoes, made in far bigger **numbers**.
The Samba was named shoe of the year, the brand's first since twenty **fifteen**.
Gulden later put it this **way**.
@gulden: I have to be very honest and confess that at the beginning of 2023, when the company's performance was not too good, it was a godsend to have the Samba and that they were in such demand.
And instead of writing the Yeezys off, Adidas sold them, for about one point four billion **euros**.
It set aside two hundred and sixty million for **charity**.
The last pair went at the end of twenty twenty-**four**. (J)
Its finance chief, Harm Ohlmeyer, put it this **way**.
@ohlmeyer: There is not one Yeezy shoe left, it has all been sold and that episode is behind us.
The forecast loss never came. Adidas closed twenty twenty-three with an operating **profit**.
On the bottom line, it still lost money for the first time since nineteen ninety-**two**.
Operating profit went from two hundred and sixty-eight million euros in twenty twenty-three to over two billion in twenty twenty-**five**.
Adidas had climbed back onto the **shelf**.

## a7 · the same week
So who actually won twenty twenty-six? First came the World **Cup**.
In the spring quarter, Adidas sold more than ever: six point seven billion **euros**.
But it spent two hundred and twelve million euros more on marketing, and its profit grew just five **percent**.
Its shoe sales grew one **percent**.
Nike's six-minute film, Rip the Script, passed sixty-six million YouTube views in under a week, the press **reported**.
It was made by Wieden and Kennedy, the agency behind Just Do **It**.
Adidas's ad stayed under seven **million**.
{wry} Nike won the attention.
Then, on Thursday the first of October, after the market closed, it reported the **quarter**. (J)
{grave} Revenue fell four percent. In Greater China, it fell twenty-**six**.
Nike told investors to expect revenue to fall again this fiscal **year**.
It also announced a plan called Pace.
It means about two and a half billion dollars of savings by twenty thirty-one, and fewer **roles**.
Nike's four regions become **three**.
{tense} On Friday morning, the stock hit its thirteen-year **low**.
It was down about eighty-one percent from its record in November twenty twenty-**one**.
Banks cut their price targets. Berenberg told clients to sell.
It said Nike seemed to have accepted a smaller place in **sportswear**.
Only about a quarter of the analysts who cover Nike still said buy, the fewest in at least twenty **years**.
Nike's chief executive, Elliott Hill, put it this **way**.
@hill: Our Nike performance business is not yet large enough to offset the pressure we are seeing in Nike sportswear, Jordan brand, and Greater China.
A month earlier, Nike had dropped out of the S and P one hundred.
It had been in that index since two thousand **eight**.
{tense} So had Adidas really won?
On Tuesday the twentieth of October, Adidas put out its numbers early.
It had made just over a billion euros of operating profit in a single quarter, the first time in its **history**.
It raised its outlook for the year to about two and a half billion euros of operating **profit**.
About two hundred million euros of it was a one-off tariff **refund**.
And its footwear sales were flat. Adidas grew on clothing and sports **gear**.
{wry} So Adidas didn't beat Nike at shoes. Nike had stepped off the **shelf**.

## a8 · the shelf is the strategy
Today, wholesale is Nike's growing channel again: sixty-one percent of brand sales in fiscal twenty twenty-**six**.
The company behind the world's most famous shoe forgot that people have to find it **first**.
And the company facing a seven-hundred-million-euro loss was saved by shoes from its own **archive**.
{warm} Here's the lesson. The shelf is the strategy. Where you sell is part of what you **sell**.
And Nike is far from finished. It has raised its dividend for twenty-four years in a **row**.
By the end of October, the stock was back above thirty-eight **dollars**.
Nike shows investors its new plan on the seventeenth of **November**.
{awe} The question is whether a company can win back a shelf it walked away **from**.
So remember that shelf from nineteen eighty-five.
Nike built an empire on being everywhere, and then decided it didn't need to be.
Adidas simply showed up wherever people **shop**.
If Nike gets its shoes back on those shelves, these were two bad years, and a company that once beat a giant will have learned its own lesson the hard way.
If it doesn't, then in the end the brothers' old rival from Bavaria may be the one still standing, because where you sell is part of what you **sell**.
Tell us in the comments: the new Nike, or the old **Samba**?
