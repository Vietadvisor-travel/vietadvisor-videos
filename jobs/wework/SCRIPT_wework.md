# wework · script (A/V, narrator supertonic:M1)
THESIS: WeWork rented for years, sold by the month and let its funder set the price.

## a0 COLD · the two accounts
A wet morning in Manhattan.
Above the traffic, strangers rent **desks**, only by the month, in an office not one of them owns.
The coffee is free, there's a beer tap in the lobby, and a sign on the wall says: do what you **love**. (J)
Adam Neumann's WeWork was an office company that told investors its mission was to elevate the world's **consciousness**. (J)
This is the story of how that company **fell**, and how its founder walked out richer.
And here's the stranger question underneath the **money**.
How do you raise money for your next company before the first one has even finished **falling**?
So we'll keep two **accounts** side by side: the company's, and the founder's.
{wry} Keep an eye on which one pays the **bills**, and which one gets paid. (J)
In August twenty twenty-two, Andreessen Horowitz, one of Silicon Valley's most famous investors, made a **bet**.
About three hundred and fifty million dollars, into Adam Neumann's new housing company, **Flow**.
It was reported as the biggest single **check** the firm had ever paid out.
But WeWork only filed for bankruptcy fifteen months **later**. [BEAT]
{tense} The comeback **started** before the crash had even finished.
Here's how that happened, and the one **decision**, signed years earlier, that made it possible.

## a1 ONE FLOOR · the trick
It began in twenty ten, when Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey started a company called **WeWork**.
They had just sold their first shared office **business**, GreenDesk, in Brooklyn.
Their first floor, at one fifty-four Grand Street in SoHo, opened in twenty **eleven**.
{warm} The trick was simple. Rent a whole floor for **years**.
Then cut it into desks, and rent the desks out by the **month**.
Add coffee, glass walls and a beer tap. Call it a **community**. (J)
For a member, that meant **freedom**: you could leave the office any month you liked, no questions asked.
But for WeWork, a lease was a promise to pay rent for years, whatever **happened**.
Remember that. It's the whole **story**.
{excited} Then the money arrived, from **Tokyo**.
In August twenty seventeen, SoftBank, run by Masayoshi Son, announced four point four billion **dollars**.
But only three billion went into WeWork itself.
The rest went to its offices in China, Japan and the **Pacific**.
So **growth** became the only number investors measured.
WeWork's desks went from about one hundred thousand, at the end of twenty sixteen, to four hundred and sixty-six thousand two years **later**.
In January twenty nineteen, SoftBank came back with two billion more, at a valuation of forty-seven billion **dollars**.
But only one billion of that was new money for the **company**.
The other billion bought shares from people who already owned **them**.
{wry} So forty-seven billion dollars wasn't a market **price**.
It was a price set by the very investor who was paying for the growth, with its own **money**.

## a2 THE SECOND ACCOUNT · what the founder took
Meanwhile, the founder's account was quietly filling with **money**.
Neumann's own company held the rights to the word "we". So WeWork paid it five point nine million dollars for **them**. (J)
The company's own filing admits he had owned stakes in buildings that WeWork **rented**.
So in some buildings, the founder was his own company's **landlord**. (J)
In April twenty nineteen, WeWork lent him three hundred and sixty-two million dollars, at two point eight nine percent, to buy its **stock**.
By that July, he had taken out about seven hundred million **dollars**.
Share sales, and loans against his stock, the Wall Street Journal reported.
{wry} The company also owned a Gulfstream jet, bought for more than sixty million **dollars**.
But here's the problem with a private account: one day, someone else gets to **read** it.

## a3 THE S-1 · the page that decided it
{tense} On Wednesday, August fourteenth, twenty nineteen, WeWork **filed** its S-1.
It's the document a company must publish before it sells shares on the stock **market**.
Outsiders could finally **read** it.
In twenty eighteen, WeWork brought in one point eight two billion dollars of **revenue**.
And it lost one point six one **billion**.
So the filing invented its own profit measure: "community-adjusted EBITDA". It left out costs like **rent**. (J)
For a company whose whole business was paying rent, that's like a bakery leaving out the **flour**. (J)
Then, on page forty-one of the filing, one line decided **everything**.
Future lease payments, already signed: forty-seven point two billion **dollars**.
Against that, contracted membership revenue of about four **billion**. [BEAT]
{grave} Years of rent on one side. Months of membership on the **other**.
That's the poison pill: rent for years, sell by the month, and let your funder set the **price**.
It also showed who was in charge. Neumann's shares carried twenty **votes** each.
And if the founder died, his wife Rebekah would help choose the next **CEO**.
So investors read it, and the forty-seven-billion price tag fell toward fifteen, then as low as **ten**.
In September, WeWork cut his votes, dropped the successor clause, and he handed back the **trademark** money.
But it wasn't enough. On Tuesday, September twenty-fourth, Adam Neumann resigned as **CEO**.
{wry} The same day, the company put the jet up for **sale**. (J)
Six days later, the stock market listing was **cancelled**.

## a4 THE RESCUE · the accounts split
{grave} And that's when the two accounts **split**, and the money went one way.
In October, SoftBank rescued WeWork. The headline said nine and a half billion **dollars**.
But only six and a half billion was for the **company**.
Three billion was an offer to buy shares from existing **owners**.
One of them was the founder, Adam **Neumann**.
His exit was reported as one point seven billion **dollars**.
In fact, about nine hundred and seventy million was for his **shares**.
Five hundred million was a credit line, which is a **loan**.
And one hundred and eighty-five million was a consulting **fee**.
Part of that deal was later cancelled, then settled on terms that were never fully **disclosed**.
{sad} The company kept the **leases**.
Then, in twenty twenty, the pandemic emptied the **offices** in every city.
But it didn't kill WeWork. WeWork lasted three more **years**.
In October twenty twenty-one, it finally reached the stock market, through a **SPAC**.
A SPAC is a shell company that's already on the stock market, so a merger skips the usual **filing**.
The headline value was nine billion **dollars**.
But the shares were valued at about six and a half **billion**.
And the cash it actually raised was about one point three **billion**.
{wry} A big headline, then a smaller real number. **Again**. (J)

## a5 THE FALL · the bill arrives
In March twenty twenty-three, WeWork announced its first month of adjusted profit, back in **December**.
{tense} Five months later, its own report warned of "substantial doubt" that it could survive as a going **concern**.
Because paying for space ate seventy-four cents of every dollar of **revenue**.
And even its buildings sat partly empty: only seventy-two percent **full**.
Its lawyers said it had already renegotiated five hundred and ninety **leases**, building by building.
{grave} Then, on Monday, November sixth, twenty twenty-three, WeWork filed for **bankruptcy**.
It had about one hundred and sixty-four million dollars in **cash**.
Against that: almost nineteen billion in liabilities on its books, more than a hundred times its **cash**.
In bankruptcy, a judge can do what no founder could: cancel **leases**.
The first batch was sixty-nine. Floor by floor, office by office, the community **emptied**.
{wry} And the founder? Fifteen months into his next **company**.
Between twenty twenty and twenty twenty-two, companies he controlled bought majority stakes in more than four thousand **apartments**.
Flow would rent them out with the same promise: not just a home, a **community**.
Andreessen Horowitz's co-founder, Marc Andreessen, explained the bet in his own **words**.
@andreessen: Adam is a visionary leader who revolutionized the second largest asset class in the world, commercial real estate.
{wry} The day that post went up, investor Jason Calacanis wrote that a startup worth a billion before it launches is "probably a **scam**". (J)

## a6 THE BID · and the other account
In early twenty twenty-four, the founder tried to buy WeWork **back**.
His offer was reported at more than five hundred million dollars, then six hundred and fifty **million**.
His letter said he was partnering with Dan Loeb's hedge fund, Third **Point**.
But Third Point said it had made no **commitment** to pay. (J)
So the creditors chose their own deal, and in May, Neumann walked **away**.
@neumann: The company looks to be emerging from bankruptcy with a plan that appears unrealistic and unlikely to succeed.
{grave} In June twenty twenty-four, WeWork came out of bankruptcy, owned mostly by Yardi, a property software **company**.
Yardi paid three hundred and thirty-seven million dollars for about sixty **percent**.
SoftBank had put in, or promised, more than ten billion before the stock market **listing**.
{excited} Then, in April twenty twenty-five, Flow raised more than one hundred million dollars **more**.
The reported value: about two and a half **billion**.
{warm} So, back to that wet morning in Manhattan. The desks are still there, rented by the month, under new **owners**.
WeWork didn't fall because of one man's ego. It fell because it rented for years and sold by the **month**.
And because the investor paying for the growth was also the one who set the **price**.
The founder survived for a simpler reason: a valuation isn't cash, and a famous name opens the next **door**.
So here's the lesson. When a founder story quotes a valuation, ask who set that price, and how much cash changed **hands**.
Flow says it may go public one day, and if it does, it will have to file its own S-1, with every number, for anyone to **read**. [BEAT]
{awe} And for the first time, we'll all finally get to read the founder's own account, line by line, in public, the way the world once read **WeWork's**.
