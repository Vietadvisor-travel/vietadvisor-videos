# lego · script (A/V, narrator supertonic:M1)
THESIS: LEGO nearly died because it stopped counting what each new part cost, and it survived because its owners could lend.

## a0 COLD · the brick that still fits
LEGO says a brick made in nineteen fifty-eight still snaps onto a brick made **today**.
That's a promise about precision, kept for more than sixty **years**. Remember that promise.
This is the story of how a company famous for precision lost count of its own **parts**, and how it got the count back.
{wry} In two thousand and three, LEGO couldn't tell you what its own boxes **cost**. (J)
One of its designers later said some sets cost more to source than the whole box sold **for**.
And no one at LEGO actually **knew**. (J)
The next year, LEGO lost almost two billion **kroner**.
Bankers advised the family to sell the **company**.
{tense} Twenty years later, LEGO was bigger than Mattel and Hasbro **combined**.
So how did LEGO nearly die? Everyone says it nearly went bankrupt, and was saved by going back to the **brick**.
The real story is stranger. It never went bankrupt, and no hit toy saved **it**. [BEAT]
It was saved by **counting**.

## a1 WOOD · the system
It started with wood, in a carpenter's workshop in the Danish village of **Billund**.
In nineteen thirty-two, Ole Kirk Christiansen began making wooden **toys**.
He took the name from two Danish words, leg godt: play **well**. (J)
His motto, from nineteen thirty-six: only the best is good **enough**.
For its first twenty-eight years, LEGO was a wood **company**.
Plastic came after the war, and LEGO's early bricks followed a British design, Kiddicraft's self-locking **brick**.
{warm} What LEGO added wasn't the brick. It was a **system**.
In nineteen fifty-five, Ole's son Godtfred launched the System of **Play**.
So every new set would work with every set a child already **owned**.
But the bricks didn't grip yet. The fix came in nineteen fifty-**eight**.
On the twenty-eighth of January, LEGO filed a patent: tubes inside the brick, so it **clutches**.
Ole Kirk died a few weeks **later**.
In February nineteen sixty, a fire hit the wooden-toy **workshop**.
{grave} Godtfred stopped making wooden toys, and his brothers **left**.
From then on LEGO made one thing: plastic bricks, in limited shapes and limited **colours**.
Remember that limit. It's what made the system **work**.

## a2 MORE · the catalogue
{excited} The system took off. By nineteen sixty-six, LEGO was sold in forty-two **countries**.
In nineteen sixty-eight, so many visitors turned up at the factory that LEGO built them a park, **LEGOLAND**.
The first season drew six hundred and twenty-five thousand people, about twice the **estimate**.
Then in nineteen seventy-eight came the **minifigure**.
For forty-six years, LEGO's worlds had had no people in **them**. (J)
In nineteen seventy-nine, the founder's grandson, Kjeld Kirk Kristiansen, took **charge**.
And by the nineties, LEGO was saying yes to **everything**.
More parks. Clothes. Watches. Video **games**. (J)
New themes, new worlds, new pieces every **year**.
Designers went from about six thousand different parts to more than twelve **thousand**.
And about ninety percent of the components were used in just one **design**.
{wry} A system where nothing gets reused isn't a system. It's a **catalogue**. (J)
Every new shape needs its own steel **mould**, and a mould can cost tens of thousands of dollars.
In nineteen ninety-eight, LEGO posted a **loss**.
In nineteen ninety-nine it signed its first outside licence: Star **Wars**.
Star Wars made money. But many of LEGO's own new worlds did **not**.

## a3 THE FALL · two bad years
{grave} Then the floor gave **way**.
Then, in two thousand and three, LEGO's sales fell by more than a **quarter**.
Its own annual report opened: two thousand and three was a very disappointing **year**. (J)
LEGO lost nine hundred and thirty-five million **kroner**.
That June, the head of strategy, Jorgen Vig Knudstorp, put a warning in **writing**.
@knudstorp: On a burning platform, losing money with negative cash flow and a real risk of debt default which could lead to a break up of the company.
He had joined LEGO less than two years **before**.
Then Kjeld took back day-to-day control, and his deputy, Poul Plougmann, was **fired**.
{tense} And two thousand and four was **worse**.
LEGO lost one billion, nine hundred and thirty-one million **kroner**.
Its main bank stopped lending, Bloomberg later **reported**.
Equity fell from about four point nine billion kroner to under three **billion**.
On the thirtieth of November two thousand and four, at a family house in the forest, bankers from Morgan Stanley met **Kjeld**.
According to one business-school case, they recommended selling LEGO, for about one point seven billion **dollars**. [BEAT]
The family said **no**.

## a4 THE COUNT · what the loss hid
But here's the twist the usual story leaves **out**.
In that worst year, the toy business itself, before write-downs, made a **profit**.
One hundred and three million **kroner**.
The giant loss came mostly from cleaning up: write-downs, restructuring, and closing what didn't **work**.
But the bricks weren't the problem. LEGO had no way to cost a single **set**.
Then designers finally checked the boxes, one by **one**.
The worst were sets with a tiny motor or fibre-optic lights: their parts cost more than the whole box sold **for**.
One study later did the maths for nineteen ninety-three to two thousand and **two**.
LEGO reported profits in almost every one of those years, yet destroyed about one point six billion dollars of **value**.
Profits on paper. Value going **down**.
That's the complexity tax: every new piece charges the whole system, the mould, the colour, the supplier, the shelf **space**.
When the tax is bigger than what a piece earns, growth makes you **poorer**.

## a5 THE CUT · the rescue
{tense} In October two thousand and four, Kjeld stepped back after twenty-five years as chief **executive**.
The new chief executive was the man who had written the **warning**.
Knudstorp was thirty-five, and the first chief executive from outside the family in a **generation**.
He said his first job wasn't strategy. It was **cash**.
@knudstorp: At first I actually said, let's not talk about strategy, let's talk about an action plan, to address the debt, to get the cash flow.
The family's holding company, KIRKBI, lent LEGO eight hundred million **kroner**.
{wry} Not a gift. A loan, with interest, due in twenty **eleven**. (J)
Costs were cut by a fifth, more than one and a half billion kroner, and about a thousand jobs **went**.
The parts library was cut back to about seven **thousand**.
So the colours were cut roughly in **half**.
And from then on, a new part had to earn its **place**.
Then the parks went **too**.
In July two thousand and five, LEGO sold control of LEGOLAND for three hundred and seventy-five million **euros**.
LEGO and its family holding company kept thirty **percent**.
{warm} In two thousand and five, LEGO made a profit again: five hundred and five million kroner, **net**.
Sales grew only a little. The swing came from what LEGO stopped **doing**.

## a6 STILL FITS · the lesson
{excited} What came next is the comeback everyone **remembers**.
In twenty fourteen, The LEGO Movie took about four hundred and sixty-eight million dollars **worldwide**.
The sequel, five years later, made less than half of **that**. (J)
But here's the thing: the movies weren't the comeback. Hits come and go, and the system is what **compounds**.
By twenty twenty-five, LEGO's revenue reached eighty-three and a half billion **kroner**.
That's nearly twelve times its two thousand and five **level**.
And year after year, between a quarter and a third of its sales came back as operating **profit**.
{warm} So, back to that brick from nineteen fifty-eight. It still **fits**.
LEGO didn't nearly die from a lack of ideas. It nearly died because it stopped counting what each idea **cost**.
And it survived because the people who owned it could afford to lend, and chose not to **sell**.
So here's the lesson: when a company you love keeps adding things, ask one question. Can anyone say what each one **costs**?
LEGO's next test isn't plastic, because its newest pieces are games, films and promises about carbon, and none of them comes in a box you can **count**. [BEAT]
{awe} Can the company that learned to count every single brick learn to count those too, before its catalogue grows too big all over **again**?
So, if you had been the family in two thousand and four, with the bankers' advice on the table: would you have sold LEGO to the highest bidder, or lent it the **money**?
Tell us in the comments, and tell us which set you'd **keep**.
